# Domain Content Map — Foundations: Information Architecture for Data Products

Read this during Phase 3 (architecture) and Phase 4 (writing) of the module
development workflow. Verify all factual claims and numbers against the
authoritative sources before publishing (Phase 2 evidence base); flag
anything disputed rather than repeating it.

## Absorbed capabilities this module must produce
1. Frame information architecture for data products through the four-systems model (organization, labeling, navigation.
2. Search) applied to analytic-specific challenges (volume-variety, exploration-monitoring splits, freshness, provenance).
3. Articulate the IA-visual-hierarchy pairing (structure deciding importance, hierarchy signaling it) grounded in task-analysis principles and the canonical Shneiderman-Few-Tufte-NN/g lineage.
4. Operate information-scent and wayfinding concepts against lost-in-hyperspace failure modes, connect architecture decisions to cognitive-load management.
5. Produce IA documentation artifacts (structure diagrams, zoning layouts, label inventories, decision records).
6. Evaluate structures through the four-question findability-orientation-prediction-mental-model heuristic with the canonical failure catalog as diagnostic lens.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Introduces the structural discipline: information architecture defined for visualization — the organizing-art thesis (the source's framing: the deliberate structuring of complex datasets into intuitive visual and navigational systems, where IA answers what content exists, how it groups, how it labels, and how users move through it — the invisible skeleton that makes visualization findable and comprehensible before any mark is drawn); the four-IA-systems model from the canonical tradition — the organization systems (the schemes and structures grouping content: the object-based, audience-based, task-based, and metaphor-based schemes with the exact-versus-ambiguous taxonomy decision), the labeling systems (the naming and representation conventions — the terminology consistency, the audience-vocabulary matching, the label-quality criteria), the navigation systems (the embedded and add-on structures moving users — the global, local, and contextual navigation triad), and the search systems (the query and retrieval interfaces — the search-versus-browse balance) as the architecture vocabulary applied to data products; the data-product-specific IA challenges — the analytic context: the volume-and-variety problem (the multi-dataset, multi-metric, multi-audience complexity that distinguishes data-product IA from website IA), the exploration-versus-monitoring split (the analytic-task diversity demanding different structures — the dashboards for glanceability, the exploratory workbenches for investigation, the reports for narrative consumption), the freshness-and-time dimensions (the live-data and temporal-navigation requirements absent from static content architecture), and the trust-and-provenance obligations (the source, method, and update-context information as first-class IA citizens) as the domain specifics; the visual-hierarchy concept — the perception-structure partner: the definition (the arrangement of elements communicating importance order — the pre-attentive guidance of attention through size, color, contrast, position, and grouping — the Gestalt and preattentive science integration from courses 1-2), the hierarchy-without-decoration insight (visual weight serving information priority, not aesthetics — the every-emphasis-earned principle), and the IA-hierarchy relationship (the architecture deciding what matters where; the hierarchy making that importance visible — the structure-and-signaling pairing) as the dual foundation; the user-task-analysis primer — the requirements source: the analytic-task taxonomy (the monitoring, discovery, investigation, and communication task families with the Shneiderman-task-list preview, the persona-task matrix mapping audiences to questions), the question-driven-structure principle (the data product's views organized around the questions users actually ask — the task-first IA methodology), and the jobs-to-be-done framing for analytics (the decision-support and understanding jobs structuring the offering) as the requirements discipline previewing section 13's process depth; the historical-and-canonical orientation — the lineage: the Shneiderman-mantra tradition (the overview-first, zoom-and-filter, details-on-demand doctrine as the analytic-navigation north star — the section-2 deep dive), the Few-dashboard doctrine (the glanceability and single-screen principles), the Tufte-layout craft (the data-ink and small-multiples traditions), and the modern analytics-UX research (the Nielsen-Norman findings on dashboard usability) as the intellectual foundations; the structural-pattern preview — the pattern library map: the overview-detail patterns, the progressive-disclosure families, the multi-view coordination architectures, the faceted-navigation systems, and the dashboard-layout conventions as the reusable structural solutions each section develops — the pattern-thinking mindset (structure decisions as pattern selections adapted to context, not inventions from zero); the information-scent-and-wayfinding concepts — the orientation mechanics: the information-scent principle (the perceptible cues indicating where information lives — the strong-scent navigation reducing hunting, the scent-of-information research lineage), the wayfinding elements (the orientation cues answering where-am-I, where-can-I-go, how-do-I-get-back — the breadcrumb, position-indicator, and landmark patterns), and the lost-in-hyperspace problem (the disorientation failure mode in deep navigation structures — the flat-structure and escape-hatch remedies) as the navigation-science foundation; the cognitive-load connection — the structural rationale: the architecture-as-load-management thesis (the IA decisions as extraneous-load reduction from course 3 — the chunked organization, the disclosed complexity, the supported working memory through external structure), the choice-overload evidence (the filter-and-navigation option proliferation degrading decisions — the curated-path discipline), and the orientation-cost economics (the navigation-cognitive-cost budgeting — the clicks-with-context versus screens-without) as the load-integration frame; the IA-documentation artifacts — the communication tools: the structure-diagram conventions (the hierarchical and network maps of views and content — the sitemap-equivalent for data products), the layout-and-wireframe systems (the zoning diagrams establishing regions and priorities, the fidelity-progression discipline), the content-and-label inventories (the metric, dimension, and terminology catalogs driving consistency), and the IA-specification practice (the documented structural decisions with rationale — the architecture-decision records for organization schemes) as the professional artifacts; the assessment-question framework — the evaluation seed: the core IA questions (can users find the information they need, do they understand where they are, can they predict where actions lead, does the structure match their mental model — the four-question evaluation heuristic previewing section 13's methods) and the common-failure recognition (the pitfalls previewed: flat-emphasis everything-important dashboards, buried disclosure, accumulation-grown layouts, context-free drill-downs, skipped-IA development) as the diagnostic lens; and the course map from the Shneiderman mantra through visual hierarchy mechanics, layout systems, dashboard architecture, progressive disclosure, navigation and wayfinding, multi-view coordination, filter and search systems, small multiples, annotation layers, design systems, the IA process, pattern case studies, and the capstone structural design.
