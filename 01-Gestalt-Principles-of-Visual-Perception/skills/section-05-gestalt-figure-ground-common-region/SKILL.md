# Figure-Ground, Common Region, and the Layered Canvas — Professional Training

## Mission

Produce professional-grade learning modules and training materials on **figure-Ground, Common Region, and the Layered Canvas** within Perceptual psychology applied to data visualization — how the brain automatically groups shapes, identifies patterns, and recognizes anomalies, and how designers engineer those automatic processes deliberately.

Subject scope: Covers the depth-and-containment principles: figure-ground organization — the stratification principle: every visual field splitting into figure (object-like, shaped, in-front, attended) and ground (background-like, unshaped, behind, ignored) with the documented determinants (smaller area, convexity, symmetry, enclosure, lower position, and motion favoring figure status; the classic Rubin-vase bistability demonstrating assignment as construction not reading), making figure-ground the layering engine of all composition; the visualization application — the mark-versus-context split: data marks as figure against chart ground (the background, grid, and frame as the receding layer — the data-ink ratio argument restated as figure-ground engineering), the annotation hierarchy (callouts as secondary figures over data figures — the z-order craft), and the ground-quality requirements (grounds must be boring — texture, contrast, or structure in backgrounds stealing figure status, the wallpaper-versus-competition discipline); the layered-graphics reality — the multi-figure problem: complex displays requiring several simultaneous figure layers (data, reference lines, annotations, highlights, controls) with the layer-separation toolkit (the documented techniques: luminance and saturation stepping between layers, the blur-and-dim depth cues, edge-quality conventions — crisp figures over soft grounds, and the interactive layer promotion — hover raising elements to figure status) as the z-axis-of-attention craft; common region — the containment law: elements inside the same bounded area grouping regardless of proximity or similarity (the Palmer-Rock demonstrations establishing common region as a dominant modern addition — the documented finding that shared regions override proximity and even resist similarity conflicts), making containers the strongest explicit grouping device; region-engineering practice — the containment craft: the card-and-panel systems in dashboards (the container hierarchy — nested regions signaling nested membership), the background-tinting technique (the softest region signal — subtle fills grouping without borders, the weight-graded containment vocabulary from tint to hairline to border to shadow), and the region-cost discipline (every container adding non-data ink and fragmentation risk — the container-crutch pitfall with the spacing-first hierarchy: gaps before tints before lines before boxes); the frame-and-margin system — the canvas structure: chart frames, panel borders, and page margins as ground-defining regions (the framed-chart versus frameless-modern styles and their perceptual consequences — frames aiding ground-separation at data-ink cost), the padding grammar (region interior spacing signaling content relationship to container), and the bleed-and-edge behaviors (elements crossing region boundaries as deliberate figure-ground violation for emphasis); the interaction with proximity-similarity — the cue-stack analysis: the documented precedence ordering (common region over proximity over similarity in the classic conflicts) with the design exploitation (using the strongest cue for the most important grouping, the hierarchy-of-cues planning) and the conflict-audit practice (mapping which cue carries each intended group); the table-and-matrix application — the dense-structure case: cell grouping in tables and heatmaps (the zebra-striping debate read as common-region versus similarity conflict — the documented banding findings, the border-versus-spacing research confirming spacing superiority at low density and borders' return at high density) as the applied cue-selection; figure-ground failures — the diagnostic layer: the bistability traps (ambiguous layers flickering between readings — the annotation-that-becomes-data problem), ground-competition (busy backgrounds fragmenting figure coherence), the reversed-figure error (emphasis styling making the context louder than the data — the highlight-dimming inversion), and the region-leak (grouping crossing container boundaries through strong similarity — the cross-card color-echo problem); the validation practice — the layer audit: the squint-test reading (which layer holds figure status under blur), the element-extraction test (do intended groups survive isolation), and the naive-viewer layer-report protocol as the figure-ground verification routine; and the figure-ground deliverable — the layered, contained, depth-structured canvas engineering.

Write as an experienced practitioner, not as a summarizer of popular content. Every framework taught must be something a real team or professional could run: procedures they can execute, criteria they can judge with, and artifacts they can hand to a colleague. Do not present claims as settled when the field treats them as contested — the training must model evidence discipline.

The module deepens this section's capabilities for a learner progressing from competent beginner toward expert practitioner, and connects them to the surrounding discipline rather than teaching them in isolation.

## Core Output Requirements

- Deliverables are Markdown documents: the module, exercise sets with model solutions, and checklists. No placeholders, no "TODO" sections.
- Ground content in authoritative sources:
- Wertheimer, Koffka, and Kohler's classical Gestalt psychology (laws of perceptual organization, Pragnanz) as the founding tradition
- Palmer, Rock, and Ehrenstein's modern re-analysis of gestalt principles (the new gestalt grouping factors: convexity, symmetry, parallelism, common-region research)
- Ware, Information Visualization: Perception for Design — the applied-perception canon bridging vision science and interface design
- Treisman's feature-integration theory and the visual-search literature (pop-out, conjunction search, anomaly detection)
- Cleveland and McGill's graphical-perception experiments (the elementary-task accuracy hierarchy) as the quantitative complement to grouping theory
- Tufte's information-design doctrine (data-ink, chartjunk, small multiples) read through perceptual-grouping lenses
- Nielsen Norman Group and applied-UX research on proximity, similarity, and common-region effects in interface layouts
- Maintain an evidence ledger while writing: every factual claim or number is either sourced, flagged as disputed/popular account, or omitted. Never invent statistics, studies, or citations.
- Distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations, and say which is which.
- Explain each specialized term in clear language on first use.

## Writing Standards

Throughout the material, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without becoming jargon-heavy
* Practical without discarding rigor
* Honest about limitations and contested findings without being defeatist
* Concrete — anchored in real cases, real artifacts, and verifiable numbers
* Progressive from fundamentals to expert judgment

Where a recommendation depends on context, explain the trade-off rather than presenting an absolute rule.

## Anti-Patterns

Avoid: Teaching the laws as a memorized list (proximity, similarity, closure) without the underlying perceptual-organization problem they solve, producing decoration-by-checklist instead of engineered perception; Ignoring accidental grouping — unintended alignments, adjacency, and shared styling creating false patterns and phantom relationships viewers cannot unsee; Over-grouping dense dashboards into cards and boxes until the containers carry more ink and attention than the data; Confusing grouping salience with importance — strong gestalt structure on the wrong element directing attention away from the decision-relevant signal; Assuming universality — grouping strengths vary with expertise, culture, and task; what pops for the designer who knows the answer may not pop for a first-time viewer; exercises without evaluation criteria; modules that stop at inspiration without a single decision the learner can now make better; and any content that overstates certainty beyond what the evidence supports.

## Decision Heuristic

When allocating depth under time or length limits, ask:

1. Where will this audience actually stall in practice?
2. Which unit protects the most value if taught well?
3. Can the learner run the framework tomorrow without me?
4. Is every example doing work a plain sentence could not?
5. What would a skeptical domain expert say about this material?

If two topics compete for space, keep the one that changes a decision.

## Final Principle

The goal is not more content about Gestalt perception. The goal is that a practitioner could take this material and perform: Engineer figure-ground stratification with documented determinant knowledge (area, convexity, symmetry, enclosure, motion) making data figure and context boring ground, diagnose figure-ground failures (bistability traps, ground competition, reversed emphasis, region leaks) through squint and extraction audits — with the same evidence discipline the field's best practitioners use. The module must function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology.
