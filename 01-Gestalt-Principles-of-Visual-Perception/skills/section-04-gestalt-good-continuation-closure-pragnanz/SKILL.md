# Good Continuation, Closure, and Pragnanz: The Completion Principles — Professional Training

## Mission

Produce professional-grade learning modules and training materials on **good Continuation, Closure, and Pragnanz: The Completion Principles** within Perceptual psychology applied to data visualization — how the brain automatically groups shapes, identifies patterns, and recognizes anomalies, and how designers engineer those automatic processes deliberately.

Subject scope: Covers the continuity-and-completion family: good continuation (the continuity law) — the alignment principle: elements arranged on a smooth path or line binding into a single continuing structure (the documented preference for collinear and co-circular groupings, the cusp-versus-smooth-intersection demonstrations), with the visualization applications (line-chart series crossing legibility — continued lines winning over switched ones at intersections, the direct-labeling placement preserving path continuity, aligned small-multiples reading as one system, the scattered-points-on-a-curve binding into pattern); occlusion and amodal completion — the behind-the-object effect: continued contours completing behind occluders (the classic overlapping-shape demonstrations) as the mechanism making layered graphics readable (annotations over data, overlapping scatter points, legend intrusions) with the design obligations (occluder clarity — clean edges signaling overlap rather than merging, the contrast and outline conventions preserving figure status); closure — the gap-completion principle: incomplete contours and shapes perceived as whole (the fragmented-circle and dotted-polygon demonstrations) with the applications (implied boundaries replacing drawn borders — the data-ink economy of closure: letting aligned element edges imply containers, dashed and dotted enclosures completing mentally, the partial-grid table designs where closure supplies missing rules) and the load-limits (closure weakening with gap size and fragment count — the completion-failure conditions); Pragnanz (the good-figure law) — the master principle: organizations tending toward the simplest, most regular, symmetric, stable interpretation available (the historical umbrella law), with the modern reading as the economy principle of perceptual inference (the minimum-complexity parse winning) explaining why ambiguous displays resolve predictably and why simple structures parse instantly; the simplicity trade-off — the design tension: Pragnanz favoring simple parses that may be wrong parses (the regularity-bias errors — viewers completing patterns the data does not have: implied trend-lines through noisy scatter, phantom gridlines from aligned points, the completion-confabulation risk) requiring deliberate disruption of false simplicity (jitter, honest irregularity, the anti-closure techniques for noisy data); the continuation-versus-proximity interaction — the competition case: the documented crossing displays where alignment and distance disagree (the X-pattern readings) with the resolution patterns (good continuation typically dominating at intersections) informing line-crossing and label-placement decisions; smoothness engineering — the craft layer: the curvature-continuity practices (the bezier smoothness conventions in line rendering, the corner-radius consistency signaling family membership, the alignment-laser discipline — sub-pixel misalignments breaking continuation), with the tooling reality (vector rendering, grid systems, optical-alignment correction); the pattern-completion connection — the anomaly link: Pragnanz-driven completion establishing the expected regularity whose violation triggers anomaly detection (the section-8 preview: the surprising-element effect depending on the strength of the completed pattern) as the pattern-and-anomaly mechanics; the evidence layer — the experimental status: the classical demonstrations' modern quantifications (the contour-integration psychophysics — the association-field findings, the closure-threshold measurements, the simplicity-preference rankings) with the computational-model connections (the field-theory mathematics — the course-10 geometry preview) as the depth literacy; failure patterns — the anti-craft: the broken-continuity error (misaligned series segments reading as separate objects, the disconnected-legend syndrome), the over-closure error (gaps so large viewers complete wrongly — the misleading-implied-boundary), and the false-Pragnanz error (regularized smoothing hiding real irregularity — the beautification-of-data trap); and the completion deliverable — the continuity-engineered, closure-economical, simplicity-aware structural design.

Write as an experienced practitioner, not as a summarizer of popular content. Every framework taught must be something a real team or professional could run: procedures they can execute, criteria they can judge with, and artifacts they can hand to a colleague. Do not present claims as settled when the field treats them as contested — the training must model evidence discipline.

The module deepens this section's capabilities for a learner progressing from competent beginner toward expert practitioner, and connects them to the surrounding discipline rather than teaching them in isolation.

## Core Output Requirements

- Deliverables are Markdown documents: the module, exercise sets with model solutions, and checklists. No placeholders, no "TODO" sections.
- Ground content in authoritative sources:
- Wertheimer, Koffka, and Kohler's classical Gestalt psychology (laws of perceptual organization, Pragnanz) as the founding tradition
- Palmer, Rock, and Ehrenstein's modern re-analysis of gestalt principles (the new gestalt grouping factors: convexity, symmetry, parallelism, common-region research)
- Ware, Information Visualization: Perception for Design — the applied-perception canon bridging vision science and interface design
- Treisman's feature-integration theory and the visual-search literature (pop-out, conjunction search, anomaly detection)
- Cleveland and McGill's graphical-perception experiments (the elementary-task accuracy hierarchy) as the quantitative complement to grouping theory
- Tufte's information-design doctrine (data-ink, chartjunk, small multiples) read through perceptual-grouping lenses
- Nielsen Norman Group and applied-UX research on proximity, similarity, and common-region effects in interface layouts
- Maintain an evidence ledger while writing: every factual claim or number is either sourced, flagged as disputed/popular account, or omitted. Never invent statistics, studies, or citations.
- Distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations, and say which is which.
- Explain each specialized term in clear language on first use.

## Writing Standards

Throughout the material, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without becoming jargon-heavy
* Practical without discarding rigor
* Honest about limitations and contested findings without being defeatist
* Concrete — anchored in real cases, real artifacts, and verifiable numbers
* Progressive from fundamentals to expert judgment

Where a recommendation depends on context, explain the trade-off rather than presenting an absolute rule.

## Anti-Patterns

Avoid: Teaching the laws as a memorized list (proximity, similarity, closure) without the underlying perceptual-organization problem they solve, producing decoration-by-checklist instead of engineered perception; Ignoring accidental grouping — unintended alignments, adjacency, and shared styling creating false patterns and phantom relationships viewers cannot unsee; Over-grouping dense dashboards into cards and boxes until the containers carry more ink and attention than the data; Confusing grouping salience with importance — strong gestalt structure on the wrong element directing attention away from the decision-relevant signal; Assuming universality — grouping strengths vary with expertise, culture, and task; what pops for the designer who knows the answer may not pop for a first-time viewer; exercises without evaluation criteria; modules that stop at inspiration without a single decision the learner can now make better; and any content that overstates certainty beyond what the evidence supports.

## Decision Heuristic

When allocating depth under time or length limits, ask:

1. Where will this audience actually stall in practice?
2. Which unit protects the most value if taught well?
3. Can the learner run the framework tomorrow without me?
4. Is every example doing work a plain sentence could not?
5. What would a skeptical domain expert say about this material?

If two topics compete for space, keep the one that changes a decision.

## Final Principle

The goal is not more content about Gestalt perception. The goal is that a practitioner could take this material and perform: Deploy good continuation for multi-series legibility (intersection readings, label placement, alignment discipline) with occlusion and amodal-completion management in layered graphics, apply closure as data-ink economy (implied boundaries, dashed enclosures, partial grids) within gap-size load limits, reason through Pragnanz as the simplicity-preference master principle predicting ambiguous-display resolution, prevent false-completion errors (phantom trends, implied regularities) through deliberate disruption techniques, and ground practice in contour-integration psychophysics with smoothness-engineering craft and broken-continuity failure prevention — with the same evidence discipline the field's best practitioners use. The module must function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology.
