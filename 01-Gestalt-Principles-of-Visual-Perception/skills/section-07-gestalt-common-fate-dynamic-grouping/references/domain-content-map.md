## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the temporal dimension of organization: common fate — the motion-binding law: elements moving in the same direction and speed binding into a group (the classical demonstrations — the moving-dot clusters, the streaming effects) with the documented dominance (motion grouping overriding static proximity and similarity when present — the strongest dynamic cue) making animation the most powerful grouping instrument and the most dangerous; the motion-perception machinery — the mechanism layer: the low-level motion detectors (the direction-selective processing — the Reichardt-detector and motion-energy lineage at awareness depth), the aperture and correspondence problems (how vision solves which-moves-where across frames), and the motion-aftereffects as evidence of channel-based processing, grounding why coherent motion binds irresistibly; animated-transitions in visualization — the object-continuity craft: the documented finding that smooth transitions preserve object identity across state changes (the tweening principle: elements morphing rather than jumping maintain the perceptual object-file — the animation-as-explanation research: transitions teaching the data transformation), making enter-update-exit animation (the D3 transition model — the course-5 tooling preview) perceptual-continuity engineering not decoration; the dynamic-grouping applications — the working cases: the brushing-and-linked-highlighting dynamics (co-moving highlights binding related elements across panels — the cross-view connection through synchronized motion), the streaming-data displays (the real-time charts where motion direction encodes time-flow and co-moving series group), the particle and flow animations (the coordinated-movement field visualization), and the state-transition storytelling (the animated reordering — the sorted-bar-race dynamics where common fate tracks identities through transformation); the motion-hierarchy design — the choreography layer: staggered versus simultaneous animation (the sequencing craft directing parse order — staggered motion creating reading sequence), the duration-and-easing grammar (the documented timing conventions — fast-enough-for-fluency, slow-enough-for-tracking ranges, the ease-in-out naturalness findings), and the motion-budget discipline (animation as high-cost high-power resource — the restraint principle: motion only where continuity or binding needs it); vestibular and accessibility constraints — the safety layer: the documented motion-sensitivity disorders (vestibular-trigger risks from large-scale motion, parallax, and zoom effects), the prefers-reduced-motion media-query obligation (the static-fallback design requirement — the WCAG animation criteria, the course-9 integration), and the seizure-threshold guidance (the flashing-frequency limits) as the non-negotiable constraints; the temporal-grouping extensions — the time-as-common-fate analogy: elements sharing temporal patterns grouping in perception (the synchronized time-series reading as trend-families, the co-occurrence binding in animation frames) with the small-multiple temporal alignment (shared time axes as the static common-fate scaffold) as the non-motion dynamic-grouping equivalent; the flicker and change-detection dynamics — the transient layer: the change-detection mechanisms (the motion-onset capture of attention — the preattentive motion channel from course 2, the change-blindness findings when transients are absent — the implication that updates without motion cues go unseen) informing real-time dashboard update design (the flash-highlight conventions for changed values); the animation-failure patterns — the anti-craft: the continuity-break errors (jump cuts destroying object identity — the un-animated reorder confusion), the motion-overload (multiple simultaneous animations competing — the choreography failure), the decorative-motion trap (animation without continuity or binding function — the attention-tax), and the infinite-motion fatigue (persistent ambient animation exhausting the motion channels — the loop-fatigue findings); the dynamic-validation practice — the motion audit: the transition-comprehension test (do viewers track identities through the animation), the reduced-motion-equivalence check (does the static version carry the same meaning), and the motion-budget review (every animation justified by continuity or binding function) as the verification routine; and the dynamic deliverable — the motion-engineered grouping, transitions, and updates within safety and budget constraints.
