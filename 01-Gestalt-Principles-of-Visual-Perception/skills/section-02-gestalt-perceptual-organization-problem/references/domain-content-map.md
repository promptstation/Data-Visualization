## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Builds the mechanistic foundation: the organization problem stated — the retinal image as an underdetermined two-dimensional mosaic of light intensities from which the visual system must recover stable objects, surfaces, groups, and layout, the inverse-optics problem (inferring world structure from ambiguous projections) making organization a matter of the brain applying assumptions rather than reading truth; mid-level vision — the processing stage where organization lives: between low-level feature extraction (edges, colors, orientations, motion energy) and high-level recognition (objects, faces, scenes), the grouping processes binding features into candidate structures (the Ware synthesis of the vision pipeline for interface design), with the key property that mid-level organization is largely stimulus-driven and pre-conscious; the ecological-assumptions view — why the laws exist: grouping principles as internalized regularities of the physical world (things that are near tend to belong together; surfaces continue behind occluders; light comes from above; objects move coherently), the Bayesian-brain framing (perception as probabilistic inference under natural-scene priors) making the laws empirical bet-hedging strategies rather than arbitrary rules; competing-cue integration — the combination problem: multiple grouping cues present simultaneously (proximity plus similarity plus common region) with the documented cue-strength hierarchy and integration behavior (some cues dominating — the common-region-versus-proximity findings, some combining), making real designs cue-conflict analyses rather than single-law applications; the neural and computational substrate — the mechanism layer at awareness depth: the documented physiology of grouping (the contextual modulation in visual cortex, the synchronization and gain-control theories) and computational models (the associative-field mathematics of good continuation, the field-model treatment of proximity) as the why-it-works-under-hood literacy; individual and task variation — the universality caveats: grouping strength modulated by expertise (chess-chunking and radiologist-reading analogies for chart reading), culture and reading-direction effects (the documented layout-scan asymmetries), age and clinical variation, and task-set effects (what the viewer is looking for changes what organizes — the task-driven top-down modulation) as the pitfall-of-assumed-universality antidote; the design-inference discipline — translating mechanism to practice: the perception-first design question set (what will bind? what will separate? what will pop? what will be missed?) asked of every layout before aesthetics, with the first-parse principle (the viewer's initial involuntary organization is the design's real content — subsequent reading works against or with it); demonstration literacy — the evidence format: perceptual demonstrations as the discipline's instrument (the classic ambiguous figures, bistable organizations, and grouping displays proving the constructive character of seeing), with the practice of building one's own demonstration set for design decisions (the argument-settling display); the relationship to sibling sciences — the curriculum position: grouping laws as the structural half of graphical perception (with the Cleveland-McGill quantitative-decoding half — which encodings read accurately) and the preattentive-attribute half (course 2 — which features process before attention), making this course the binding-and-structure foundation; the anomaly-preview — the pattern side completed in search science: grouping producing the expected regularity against which violations register (the section-8 depth) as the source's pattern-and-anomaly pairing; and the organization deliverable — the mechanism-grounded model of how viewers construct structure from marks.
