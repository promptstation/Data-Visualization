# Gestalt in Layout: Dashboards, Pages, and Multi-Panel Composition — Professional Training

## Mission

Produce professional-grade learning modules and training materials on **gestalt in Layout: Dashboards, Pages, and Multi-Panel Composition** within Perceptual psychology applied to data visualization — how the brain automatically groups shapes, identifies patterns, and recognizes anomalies, and how designers engineer those automatic processes deliberately.

Subject scope: Scales grouping to composition level: the layout-as-grouping problem — the page-scale question: dashboards and reports as large-scale perceptual organizations where the same laws govern panel relationships, reading paths, and information architecture (the fractal property of grouping — the section-3 scale-nesting applied to pages), making layout design applied gestalt at maximum scale; the panel-grouping system — the container architecture: the dashboard-panel binding decisions (the section-5 common-region toolkit at panel scale — the card, band, and zone systems), the panel-family formation (related panels grouping through proximity, aligned edges, shared styling — the similarity-of-treatment convention), and the hierarchy-of-zones (the primary-secondary-tertiary region structure with the visual-weight gradient) as the containment architecture; reading-path engineering — the scan-structure craft: the documented layout-scan patterns (the F-pattern and Z-path findings from web-eyetracking research, the top-left primacy in left-to-right reading cultures — the course-17 culture caveat), the path-shaping tools (the alignment-continuation channels, the size-and-weight figure hierarchy, the gaze-direction cues in imagery) with the decision-relevance ordering (the most decision-critical panel in the strongest first-fixation zone) as the scan architecture; the alignment-system discipline — the invisible grid: the document-grid as the parallelism-and-continuation backbone (the section-6 parallelism law at page scale — aligned edges binding the composition into one system), the baseline-grid practices for text-chart integration, the margin-and-gutter ratios as the proximity grammar (the section-3 spacing ratios scaled to layout), and the misalignment cost (single off-grid elements reading as errors or emphasis — the deliberate-violation discipline); the information-density management — the space-versus-structure tension: the dense-dashboard reality (proximity grouping requiring space that monitoring displays cannot spare — the section-3 density trade-off at page scale) with the compensation stack (common regions over gaps, the typography-weight grouping substitutes, the interactive progressive-disclosure — the Shneiderman-mantra preview for course 7), and the density-zoning strategy (dense monitoring regions versus spacious analysis regions in one composition); the cross-panel binding — the multi-view system: the coordinated-views grouping (the linked-highlighting as cross-panel common fate — the section-7 dynamics, the shared-dimension alignment enabling cross-panel continuation reading, the consistent-encoding requirement — same color same meaning everywhere as the similarity-binding contract) making multi-view dashboards one perceptual system rather than a panel collection; the header-furniture-chrome layer — the non-data grouping: the navigation, title, filter, and legend furniture as ground-layer elements (the figure-ground discipline — chrome receding through consistent treatment, the chrome-data boundary clarity preventing furniture from reading as content) with the title-as-grouping-label practice (panel headers as the verbal common-region markers); the responsive-grouping problem — the multi-format reality: the reflow challenges (grouping structures surviving viewport changes — the proximity-ratio preservation under resize, the panel-stack reordering logic for mobile, the breakpoint-as-grouping-boundary principle — reflow points at semantic group boundaries not arbitrary widths) as the responsive-layout perceptual discipline; the template-and-system design — the scalable composition: the layout-system approach (the grid-template libraries, the panel-component families with baked-in grouping grammar, the spacing-token systems — the design-token architecture making proximity ratios consistent by construction) as the institutionalized-gestalt practice; the layout-failure patterns — the diagnostic layer: the panel-soup (uniform spacing producing no hierarchy — the equidistant-grid trap at page scale), the chrome-competition (furniture outranking data in figure strength — the reversed-emphasis at layout level), the orphan-panel error (semantically related panels separated by grouping-destroying distance), and the path-collapse (composition offering no scan structure — the everything-important-nothing-is failure); the layout-audit practice — the composition check: the squint-test at page scale (does the zone hierarchy survive blur), the five-second-test (what does the first parse deliver — the section-3 protocol scaled), the path-tracing review (does the intended reading order match the structural cues) as the composition-verification routine; and the layout deliverable — the grouping-engineered dashboard and page architecture.

Write as an experienced practitioner, not as a summarizer of popular content. ## Core Output Requirements

- Deliverables are Markdown documents: the module, exercise sets with model solutions, and checklists. No placeholders, no "TODO" sections.
- Ground content in authoritative sources:
- Wertheimer, Koffka, and Kohler's classical Gestalt psychology (laws of perceptual organization, Pragnanz) as the founding tradition
- Palmer, Rock, and Ehrenstein's modern re-analysis of gestalt principles (the new gestalt grouping factors: convexity, symmetry, parallelism, common-region research)
- Ware, Information Visualization: Perception for Design — the applied-perception canon bridging vision science and interface design
- Treisman's feature-integration theory and the visual-search literature (pop-out, conjunction search, anomaly detection)
- Cleveland and McGill's graphical-perception experiments (the elementary-task accuracy hierarchy) as the quantitative complement to grouping theory
- Tufte's information-design doctrine (data-ink, chartjunk, small multiples) read through perceptual-grouping lenses
- Nielsen Norman Group and applied-UX research on proximity, similarity, and common-region effects in interface layouts
- Maintain an evidence ledger while writing: every factual claim or number is either sourced, flagged as disputed/popular account, or omitted. Never invent statistics, studies, or citations.
- Distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations, and say which is which.
- Explain each specialized term in clear language on first use.

## Writing Standards

Throughout the material, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without becoming jargon-heavy
* Practical without discarding rigor
* Honest about limitations and contested findings without being defeatist
* Concrete — anchored in real cases, real artifacts, and verifiable numbers
* Progressive from fundamentals to expert judgment

Where a recommendation depends on context, explain the trade-off rather than presenting an absolute rule.

## Anti-Patterns

Avoid: Teaching the laws as a memorized list (proximity, similarity, closure) without the underlying perceptual-organization problem they solve, producing decoration-by-checklist instead of engineered perception; Ignoring accidental grouping — unintended alignments, adjacency, and shared styling creating false patterns and phantom relationships viewers cannot unsee; Over-grouping dense dashboards into cards and boxes until the containers carry more ink and attention than the data; Confusing grouping salience with importance — strong gestalt structure on the wrong element directing attention away from the decision-relevant signal; Assuming universality — grouping strengths vary with expertise, culture, and task; what pops for the designer who knows the answer may not pop for a first-time viewer; exercises without evaluation criteria; modules that stop at inspiration without a single decision the learner can now make better; and any content that overstates certainty beyond what the evidence supports.

## Decision Heuristic

When allocating depth under time or length limits, ask:

1. Where will this audience actually stall in practice?
2. Which unit protects the most value if taught well?
3. Can the learner run the framework tomorrow without me?
4. Is every example doing work a plain sentence could not?
5. What would a skeptical domain expert say about this material?

If two topics compete for space, keep the one that changes a decision.
