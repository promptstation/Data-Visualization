## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Introduces the discipline and its founding insight: Gestalt psychology defined — the early-twentieth-century school (Wertheimer, Koffka, Kohler) demonstrating that perception is not built up from atomic sensations but organized wholes ("Gestalten") whose properties the parts do not possess, the classic phi-phenomenon finding (apparent motion from static alternating lights — the whole of motion existing nowhere in the parts) as the founding experiment; the whole-thesis operationalized for visualization — viewers never see raw marks (pixels, lines, dots); they see structured objects, groups, surfaces, and patterns imposed by the visual system's organization rules, so a chart is not what is drawn but what is perceived, making perceptual-organization knowledge the difference between engineering understanding and hoping for it; why automaticity matters — grouping happens before and beneath conscious deliberation (the mandatory, unswitchable character of perceptual organization), so designers cannot instruct viewers to see structure — they can only build graphics whose automatic parsing produces the intended structure, the source's "brain automatically groups shapes" claim as the course's governing fact; the visualization stakes — every data graphic is an arrangement of marks whose meaning depends on which marks the visual system binds together (bars read as pairs or as separate, scatter clouds read as clusters or noise, lines read as continuing behind occluders or stopping), making grouping laws the grammar of chart legibility; the historical arc — Gestalt theory's suppression and revival (the behaviorist-era eclipse, the mid-century cognitive-psychology recovery, the modern computational and neuroscientific treatment of perceptual organization) with the applied-perception canon (Ware's perception-for-design synthesis) as the current bridge to design practice; the law inventory previewed — proximity, similarity, good continuation, closure, figure-ground, common region, common fate, symmetry, Pragnanz as the family the course dissects one by one, taught as problem-solving responses of the visual system rather than a memorized checklist (the pitfall named); the pattern-and-anomaly frame from the source — grouping as the mechanism behind both pattern identification (bound elements read as structure) and anomaly recognition (the element violating the group's regularity pops — the visual-search science of section 8), positioning the course around the two jobs data graphics perform; the measurement tradition introduced — perceptual claims as experimental claims (the psychology lineage of controlled demonstration and quantified effect — the Cleveland-McGill task hierarchy as sibling evidence culture) requiring designers to think in testable effects; the practitioner identity — the perception-engineer profile (designer who reasons from mechanisms: which law fires here, which competes, which wins, and what the viewer's first parse will be) as the capability the course builds; and the course map from the organization problem through the classical laws, modern revisions, dynamic grouping, search and anomaly science, chart and dashboard applications, grouping failures, validation methods, and audit practice to the capstone perception-engineered visualization.
