# Domain Content Map — Business Intelligence Systems and the Modern Data Stack: Platform Literacy

Read this during Phase 3 (architecture) and Phase 4 (writing) of the module
development workflow. Verify all factual claims and numbers against the
authoritative sources before publishing (Phase 2 evidence base); flag
anything disputed rather than repeating it.

## Absorbed capabilities this module must produce
1. Navigate BI systems through landscape orientation (BI concept and reporting-analytics-self-service evolution, value chain from sources through consumption, roles-responsibility mapping with RACI clarity, platform-literacy motivation), understand data foundations (warehouse-lake-lakehouse architectures with cost-performance awareness, Kimball dimensional modeling with grain and conformed-dimension discipline, dbt-tradition transformation layers with testing and documentation, semantic layer concepts and headless BI).
2. Trace pipelines and quality (ingestion patterns across batch-streaming-CDC, quality engineering with dimensions framework and observability, lineage and documentation with impact analysis, freshness SLA management, cost operations).
3. Operate the consumption ecosystem (BI tool tiers with selection criteria, notebook analytics with productionization pathways, reverse-ETL activation, integration architecture, buy-build evaluation), connect systems to governance (access-security architecture with row-column patterns, standards and version control, center-of-excellence models, compliance-audit interfaces, trust-inheritance principle), and avoid consumption-only literacy, grain blindness, semantic absence, trust inheritance, tool chasing, documentation debt, cost blindness, and governance afterthoughts failures.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the systems literacy letting visualization specialists understand where their artifacts live and how trustworthy numbers get produced: the BI landscape orientation — what business intelligence is: the BI concept and history (the business-intelligence term spanning the technology, process, and practice of converting operational data into decision-support information, with the decision-support-systems heritage from the management-information-systems traditions; the reporting-to-analytics-to-self-service evolution where BI progressed from IT-controlled report factories through analyst tooling to business-user exploration, each phase redefining the visualization specialist's role; the modern-stack era where cloud warehouses, ELT pipelines, transformation layers, and semantic models reorganized the BI architecture around modularity and version control); the BI value chain (the source-systems layer where operational applications generate the transactional reality BI reports on; the ingestion-and-integration layer moving data through ETL and ELT patterns into analytical stores; the storage-and-computation layer where warehouses, lakes, and lakehouses hold and process analytical data; the transformation-and-modeling layer where governed business logic defines metrics and structures; the consumption layer where dashboards, reports, notebooks, and alerts deliver decision support; the value-chain literacy requirement where visualization specialists understand enough of each layer to diagnose where their problems originate); the roles and responsibilities map (the data-engineer, analytics-engineer, analyst, and visualization-specialist division of labor with its evolving boundaries; the BI-ownership models ranging from centralized teams through embedded analysts to federated centers of excellence; the stakeholder-ecosystem including business owners, data stewards, and governance bodies from section 12's systems; the RACI clarity principle where dashboard and metric production requires explicit accountability mapping across the chain); and the platform-literacy motivation (the dependency-awareness where artifact quality inherits pipeline quality, making systems understanding a communication-trust issue rather than technical trivia; the feasibility-judgment where design proposals confront implementation reality through informed estimation; the failure-diagnosis capability distinguishing data problems, model problems, and presentation problems through systematic tracing) as the orientation layer; the data foundation — warehouses, lakes, and models: the storage architectures (the data-warehouse pattern with its schema-on-read discipline, dimensional organization, and query-performance engineering; the data-lake pattern with its schema-on-write flexibility, raw-data preservation, and governance challenges; the lakehouse synthesis attempting warehouse discipline over lake economics; the cloud-platform landscape where Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift, and Databricks implementations carry shared concepts with platform-specific behaviors; the cost-and-performance awareness where query patterns in dashboards and exploration generate real monetary and latency consequences connecting to the performance traditions); the dimensional modeling heritage (the Kimball-tradition fact-and-dimension architecture where business processes map to fact tables described by shared dimensions; the star-and-snowflake schema patterns with their query and maintenance tradeoffs; the grain-definition discipline where fact table granularity determines what questions the model can answer, the root of many dashboard impossibility discoveries; the slowly-changing-dimension handling where historical accuracy in metrics depends on temporal modeling decisions; the conformed-dimensions principle enabling consistent cross-process analysis, the modeling ancestor of section 3's consistency requirements); the modern transformation layer (the ELT paradigm where raw data lands before transformation, moving business logic into version-controlled code; the dbt-tradition tooling where SQL transformations carry testing, documentation, and lineage as engineering artifacts; the model-layering conventions staging-intermediate-marts organizing transformation logic by abstraction level; the analytics-engineer role emergence formalizing the transformation craft between engineering and analysis; the test-and-documentation obligations where metric correctness inherits transformation quality assurance); and the semantic layer concept (the metrics-layer idea where governed metric definitions sit between data models and consumption tools, enforcing section 3's single-definition discipline technically; the headless-BI pattern separating metric logic from visualization rendering; the semantic-layer maturity spectrum from naming conventions through centralized metric stores; the consumption-integration where dashboard tools and notebooks query governed definitions rather than recomputing locally) as the data foundation layer; the pipeline and quality systems — how data flows and fails: the ingestion patterns (the batch-versus-streaming distinction where scheduled loads and continuous flows serve different freshness requirements from section 3's semantics; the CDC-change-data-capture patterns tracking operational modifications for historical accuracy; the API-and-file ingestion varieties with their reliability and schema-drift challenges; the pipeline-orchestration tools where Airflow-tradition schedulers manage dependency graphs, retries, and alerting); the data quality engineering (the quality-dimensions framework covering accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, and uniqueness with testable definitions; the pipeline-testing practices including schema validation, row-count monitoring, distribution checks, and freshness assertions from the transformation-layer traditions; the observability tooling where automated monitoring detects anomalies before business users do, connecting to section 3's anomaly disciplines; the incident-response workflows where data-quality failures trigger investigation, communication, and correction processes with downstream artifact implications); the lineage and documentation (the column-and-table lineage tracking where metric values trace through transformations to sources, the technical backbone of section 3's lineage obligations; the documentation-systems where data catalogs, model docs, and metric registries make the estate navigable; the impact-analysis capability where upstream changes reveal affected dashboards and reports before breakage; the knowledge-management discipline where tribal pipeline knowledge gets formalized against team turnover); the freshness and latency management (the SLA-definition practice where data-availability commitments align pipeline design with business expectations; the latency-budget allocation where end-to-end freshness decomposes across ingestion, transformation, and serving stages; the partial-data handling where in-progress periods display with explicit incompleteness markers per section 4's honesty disciplines; the stale-data escalation where SLA breaches trigger the alert and communication systems of section 14); and the cost and performance operations (the compute-cost drivers where dashboard query patterns, refresh schedules, and warehouse sizing generate spend requiring monitoring; the query-optimization collaboration where visualization specialists contribute index-friendly and aggregate-friendly design per the performance traditions; the usage-based pruning where unused models, dashboards, and pipelines face retirement through section 4's governance rituals) as the pipeline layer; the consumption ecosystem — tools and integration: the BI tool landscape (the enterprise-platform tier where Tableau, Power BI, and Qlik serve governed organizational reporting with the ecosystem-specific strengths; the modern-lightweight tier where Looker, Mode, Sigma, and Hex target semantic-integrated and notebook-fluent workflows; the embedded-and-custom tier where libraries and application-integrated visualization serve product contexts per the graphics and SVG traditions; the tool-selection criteria covering data connectivity, semantic integration, collaboration features, governance capabilities, licensing economics, and organizational skill base; the multi-tool reality where enterprises run portfolios requiring cross-tool consistency governance); the notebook and code-first analytics (the Python-and-R analysis environments where statistical work and prototyping occur before productionization; the notebook-to-dashboard pathways where exploratory findings graduate into governed artifacts through transformation and semantic layers; the reproducibility requirements connecting to the workflow traditions of the analysis courses); the reverse-ETL and activation patterns (the operational-data-return flows where analytical outputs feed business applications, marketing systems, and operational tools; the alerting-and-notification integrations where metric thresholds trigger Slack, email, and workflow systems per section 3's distribution; the data-product concept where analytical outputs ship with service-level expectations and user-experience design); the integration architecture (the API and connector landscape where consumption tools access data platforms through governed interfaces; the SSO-and-access-management integration where BI permissions inherit organizational identity systems per section 12's security; the embedding-scenarios where dashboards integrate into internal portals and customer-facing products with their distinct requirements); and the vendor and build landscape literacy (the buy-versus-build analysis for tooling decisions with total-cost-of-ownership reasoning; the vendor-evaluation process including proof-of-concept practice against real organizational data and questions; the lock-in-and-migration awareness where semantic layers and version-controlled transformations reduce platform dependency) as the consumption layer; the governance intersection — where systems meet trust: the access and security architecture (the role-based-and-attribute-based access models controlling who sees which data through which tools; the row-and-column-level security patterns serving the audience-tier architectures of section 4 without artifact duplication; the sensitive-data handling where PII and confidential business data receive classification, masking, and audit treatment per section 12's ethics); the standards and conventions (the naming-and-structure conventions where organizational standards make the data estate navigable across teams; the version-control-everything principle extending code discipline to metric definitions, dashboard configurations, and documentation; the release-and-change processes where BI modifications follow review, testing, and deployment practices from the engineering traditions); the center-of-excellence patterns (the enablement-function design where central teams provide platforms, standards, training, and consulting rather than producing every artifact; the self-service-support model where business analysts build within governed frameworks, detailed in section 10; the maturity-model thinking where BI capability development follows staged progression from reporting through optimization); the compliance and audit interfaces (the regulatory-reporting dependencies where financial, privacy, and industry regulations impose data and documentation requirements; the audit-trail capabilities where access logs, change histories, and lineage support investigation and assurance; the records-management obligations where decision-support artifacts carry retention and archival requirements); and the systems-to-communication linkage (the trust-inheritance principle where every dashboard claim rests on pipeline quality, model correctness, and governance discipline, making systems literacy a communication-ethics competency from section 12; the diagnosis-vocabulary where visualization specialists describe problems precisely enough for engineering collaboration; the architecture-informed-design where systems constraints and capabilities shape artifact possibilities realistically) as the governance layer; and the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the consumption-only literacy, designing artifacts without understanding the systems producing their data until failures exceed diagnostic vocabulary, remedied by the value-chain orientation; the grain-blindness, promising dashboard detail the dimensional model cannot support, remedied by the grain-definition literacy; the semantic-layer-absence, every tool recomputing metrics locally until contradictory numbers erode trust, remedied by the governed definitions integration; the pipeline-trust-inheritance, assuming upstream quality without verification or monitoring, remedied by the quality-engineering collaboration; the tool-fashion-chasing, adopting platforms without selection criteria or migration analysis, remedied by the evaluation-process discipline; the documentation-debt, undocumented models and metrics accumulating tribal knowledge until turnover breaks operations, remedied by the documentation obligations; the cost-blindness, dashboard refresh patterns generating surprise warehouse spend, remedied by the cost-operations monitoring; and the governance-afterthought, bolting access controls and standards onto mature estates at multiples of design-time cost, remedied by the architecture-integrated governance with detection methods as the diagnostic.
