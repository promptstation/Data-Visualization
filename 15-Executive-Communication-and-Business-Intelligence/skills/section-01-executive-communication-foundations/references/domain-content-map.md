# Domain Content Map — Executive Communication Foundations: The Decision-Maker Audience

Read this during Phase 3 (architecture) and Phase 4 (writing) of the module
development workflow. Verify all factual claims and numbers against the
authoritative sources before publishing (Phase 2 evidence base); flag
anything disputed rather than repeating it.

## Absorbed capabilities this module must produce
1. Master the executive audience through profile analysis (time-scarcity economics with decision orientation, cognitive style variety demanding layered multimodal materials, organizational position effects including delegation chains, stakeholder mapping and preference research).
2. Classify communication purposes (decision-support with options-recommendation patterns and decision-quality standards, monitoring with exception alerting, alignment and strategy cascade, honest advocacy with persuasion boundaries, single-purpose discipline with mismatch diagnosis), navigate reception psychology (primacy-recency dynamics with middle valleys, confirmation and motivated reasoning navigation, risk-loss perception patterns, trust accumulation-destruction asymmetries, emotional-relational layer).
3. Operate within the communication ecosystem (artifact landscape coherence with single-source discipline, meeting-calendar contexts with pre-read cultures, organizational information flows across upward-horizontal-downward-informal channels, channel-medium landscape), and avoid audience confusion, purpose drift, attention overestimation, confirmation battles, trust spending, and ecosystem blindness failures.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the audience analysis that makes executive communication a distinct discipline: the executive audience profile — who receives the work: the role and attention reality (the time-scarcity economics where executives allocate minutes rather than hours to any single analysis, making compression a survival skill for the communicator; the decision-orientation where executives consume information to act, delegate, approve, or defer rather than to learn for its own sake, the fundamental difference from analytic audiences; the portfolio-attention context where the data topic competes with dozens of other organizational concerns, requiring immediate relevance signaling; the experience-and-intuition layer where pattern recognition from decades of decisions shapes how evidence is weighed alongside analysis); the cognitive style variety (the analytical versus intuitive decision-maker spectrum requiring different evidence presentation densities; the visual versus verbal preference variation across individuals, demanding multimodal redundancy in executive materials; the detail-orientation differences where some executives drill into numbers and others resist them, requiring layered materials serving both; the reading-behavior reality where executive documents get scanned rather than read, structuring everything that follows); the organizational position effects (the authority and accountability context where executives own consequences of decisions the analysis informs, raising the stakes of communication accuracy; the political landscape awareness where information flows carry organizational meaning beyond content, previewing the trust systems of section 12; the delegation chains where executive communications get relayed and reinterpreted down the organization, demanding clarity that survives transmission); and the audience research discipline (the stakeholder mapping practice identifying who decides, who influences, who executes, and who must be informed for each communication; the preference elicitation through direct observation and feedback rather than assumption; the relationship history awareness where past communications shape current reception) as the audience foundation; the communication purpose taxonomy — what executives need: the decision-support purpose (the options-with-recommendation pattern where analysis exists to select among alternatives, requiring tradeoff clarity and consequence forecasting; the go-no-go evaluations where binary decisions need threshold reasoning and risk framing; the resource-allocation decisions where comparative returns and opportunity costs structure the argument; the decision-quality standards where good decisions from good processes may still yield bad outcomes, distinguishing decision quality from outcome quality in the evaluation logic); the monitoring purpose (the status-awareness communications keeping executives informed about ongoing operations without demanding action, the dashboard and report territory of sections 3 and 4; the exception-alerting patterns where only deviations from expectation warrant executive attention, the management-by-exception principle; the situational-awareness building where cumulative monitoring communications create the mental model executives use in crisis, previewing section 11); the alignment purpose (the strategy-cascade communications translating organizational direction into measurable terms, connecting to the measurement systems of section 3; the change-support communications where data builds the case for organizational transitions; the culture-shaping communications where repeated data rituals influence how the organization thinks, previewing section 13); the persuasion and advocacy purposes (the upward-influence patterns where analysts advocate for investments, priorities, or corrections through evidence; the external-communication contexts where executive data presentations serve boards, investors, and partners with elevated stakes and legal exposure; the honest-advocacy boundary where recommendation differs from manipulation, applying the rhetorical ethics traditions from the semiographics course); and the purpose-clarity discipline (the single-primary-purpose rule where each communication artifact serves one dominant purpose with secondary purposes explicitly subordinated; the purpose-mismatch diagnosis where monitoring materials get used for decisions and decision briefs get filed as reports, the common failure mode of undifferentiated communication) as the purpose layer; the psychology of executive reception — how messages land: the primacy and recency dynamics (the opening-seconds judgment where executives form working conclusions immediately and process subsequent evidence through that frame, motivating answer-first structures detailed in section 2; the closing-action-orientation where final content shapes what happens next, demanding explicit ask-and-next-step endings; the middle-attention-valley where supporting evidence competes for diminishing attention, requiring structural signposting); the confirmation and motivated reasoning (the prior-belief-filtering where executives weight evidence supporting existing positions more heavily, a bias the communicator must navigate honestly rather than exploit, from the decision-science traditions; the sunk-commitment effects where past decisions shape data reception, requiring diplomatic handling of contradicting evidence; the disconfirming-evidence-presentation craft where challenging executive beliefs succeeds through respect, sourcing, and incremental framing rather than surprise attack); the risk and loss perception (the loss-aversion asymmetry where threat framing motivates more than opportunity framing, with the ethical usage boundaries from the persuasion traditions; the probability-perception patterns where executives intuitively weight risks differently from statistical reality, requiring the uncertainty communication skills of section 11; the magnitude-sensitivity where large numbers compress and small numbers dismiss unless scaled to organizational relevance through per-unit and comparison framing); the trust dynamics preview (the credibility-accumulation where consistent accuracy over time buys attention and belief for future communications, the compounding asset detailed in section 12; the credibility-destruction asymmetry where single overclaims or discovered errors cost disproportionate trust; the source-relationship effects where the messenger's organizational standing shapes message reception independent of content quality); and the emotional and relational layer (the respect-signaling where communication craft itself demonstrates professional regard for the audience's time and intelligence; the confidence-calibration where appropriate certainty language builds trust while both overconfidence and excessive hedging undermine it; the relationship-maintenance through communication consistency, cadence reliability, and follow-through on commitments made in briefings) as the psychology layer; the executive communication ecosystem — the context system: the artifact landscape (the standing instruments including dashboards, scorecards, and periodic reports providing continuous context; the episodic instruments including briefs, decks, and memos serving specific decisions and updates; the conversational instruments including briefings, check-ins, and corridor exchanges where much executive information actually flows; the artifact-ecosystem coherence requirement where instruments must tell consistent stories with consistent numbers, the single-source-of-truth discipline previewing section 5's systems); the meeting and calendar contexts (the standing-meeting slots with fixed formats and time budgets shaping artifact design; the pre-read cultures where materials arrive before discussion versus live-presentation cultures, requiring different document completeness; the decision-meeting versus update-meeting versus brainstorm distinctions with their different communication contracts; the calendar-scarcity reality where earning meeting time requires demonstrated value per minute); the organizational information flows (the upward-reporting chains where analysis travels through management layers with transformation risks at each relay; the horizontal-coordination needs where peer executives receive consistent versions preventing contradiction discovery in meetings; the downward-cascade where executive communications get translated for broader organizations, demanding transmission-robust clarity; the informal-network reality where data narratives spread through organizational conversation, shaping interpretation of formal communications); and the channel and medium landscape (the synchronous versus asynchronous distribution with their different preparation and completeness demands, detailed in section 14; the document versus slide versus dashboard medium selection by purpose and culture, previewing sections 4, 6, and 7; the mobile and remote consumption reality where executive materials increasingly arrive on phones between meetings, demanding responsive design from the mobile traditions; the channel-formality gradient where instant messages and emails carry organizational signals alongside content) as the ecosystem layer; and the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the analyst-audience confusion, presenting to executives as if they were data-team peers with methodology depth they neither need nor want, remedied by the audience-profile discipline; the purpose drift, artifacts accumulating content until no clear purpose remains actionable, remedied by the single-primary-purpose rule; the attention overestimation, designing for reading time the audience will never spend, remedied by the scan-first design and time-budget realism; the confirmation-battle, confronting executive priors with blunt contradicting evidence triggering defensive rejection, remedied by the disconfirming-evidence craft; the trust-spending overclaim, confident language on weak data buying short-term persuasion at compounding credibility cost, remedied by the calibration discipline of section 12; and the ecosystem blindness, optimizing one artifact while contradicting numbers circulate elsewhere, remedied by the ecosystem coherence and single-source discipline with detection methods as the diagnostic.
