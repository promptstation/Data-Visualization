1. Scale accessible visualization organizationally by establishing policy and strategy (written commitments with enforceable tiers, assessment-to-roadmap cycles, legal-procurement alignment, resource models), building the people architecture (center-of-excellence, champion, and hybrid models, role-specific responsibilities across design-development-product-QA-data, tiered competency development, incentive structures with disabled professional inclusion), governing through design systems as the primary lever (verified component libraries with release gates, accessible token distribution, do-don't-verify documentation with rationale, maintenance and migration discipline), integrating process across the lifecycle (shift-left requirements-design-development-test gates, agile definition-of-done inclusion, release checklists with exception governance, incident response, supply chain verification), instrumenting accountability (coverage-quality-experience-leading metric sets, executive-team-external reporting, gaming-resistant targets, maturity benchmarking), cultivating culture (limits-respecting empathy, guilds and rituals, advocacy coalitions, hero-independent sustainability), and preventing policy-without-teeth, ivory-tower, bypass, checkbox, hero-dependence, debt-spiral, vendor-blindness, and metric-theater failures.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the organizational scaling layer: the policy and strategy foundation — the institutional commitment: the accessibility policy architecture (the written policy statement adopting WCAG conformance targets from the standards of section 1, the translation from policy to enforceable requirements with aspirational guidance clearly separated, and the executive sponsorship that places ownership at leadership level rather than volunteer level); the strategy development cycle (the current state assessment through the audits and maturity baselines of section 12, the target state definition setting conformance levels by product criticality and audience, and the phased roadmap that sequences remediation of existing debt against prevention in new work); the legal and market alignment (the ADA, European Accessibility Act, and EN 301-549 obligation mapping from section 1 expressed as concrete policy requirements, and the procurement posture recognizing that conformance statements are competitive necessities in public sector and enterprise sales); and the resource commitment decisions (the staffing models ranging from dedicated accessibility teams to embedded responsibility with central support, and the tooling investment covering scanner licenses, assistive technology, and testing platforms from section 13) as the foundation layer; the people architecture — roles, skills, and incentives: the organizational models (the center of excellence providing expertise, standards, and tooling to delivery teams, the distributed champion network placing a trained lead in every team, and the hybrid that combines central standards with embedded execution as the most common mature pattern); the role-specific responsibilities (designers owning pattern compliance and contrast and cognitive checks from sections 6 and 8, developers owning semantics, keyboard behavior, and component testing from sections 4, 7, and 11, product managers including accessibility in requirements and prioritization, QA and research executing the testing triad of section 12, and data and analytics teams producing descriptions and table equivalents from section 5); the competency development system (the tiered training curriculum from awareness through expert practice, the professional certification paths, and the internal knowledge infrastructure of wikis, pattern libraries, and office hours); and the incentive structures (accessibility goals integrated into performance reviews, expertise valued as a career specialization, and the hiring and retention of disabled professionals strengthening internal understanding under the nothing-about-us principle) as the people layer; the design system integration — the primary scaling lever: the component library strategy (the insight that verified components propagate compliance fleet-wide, making the design system the highest-leverage accessibility investment, the per-chart-type accessibility specifications of section 9 documented as component requirements, and the release gates requiring accessibility evidence for every design system contribution); the token governance (the CVD-safe and contrast-verified color palettes of section 6 distributed as design tokens, spacing, typography, and motion tokens carrying the cognitive and low-vision supports of section 8, and theme variants including dark mode and high contrast verified as a matrix rather than afterthoughts); the documentation standard (the do, don't, and verify guidance per component, the implementation recipes with testing instructions from section 13, and the rationale explaining principles so teams can handle cases the patterns did not anticipate); and the system maintenance (the regression prevention through design system CI, the deprecation and migration governance that propagates accessibility improvements to legacy usage, and the contribution review process for new patterns) as the pattern layer; the process integration — accessibility in the delivery lifecycle: the shift-left practices (accessibility criteria written into user stories and acceptance conditions at requirements time, pattern compliance and review gates at design time, component standards and linting at development time, and triad execution at test time, following the lifecycle of section 12); the agile integration (incremental compliance work inside sprint cycles with accessibility in the definition of done, backlog management that weights remediation debt by user impact, and inclusive research practices inside product discovery); the release governance (launch checklists with accessibility gates, accessibility statement updates as launch artifacts, and the exception process requiring documented justification, compensating measures, remediation plans, and executive sign-off); the incident management (severity classification and response commitments for accessibility bugs, the user-reported barrier handling with feedback closure from section 12, and continuous monitoring through field telemetry); and the supply chain governance (procurement requirements including VPAT review and contractual obligations, third-party component and embedded visualization assessment, and verification of agency and contractor deliverables against internal standards) as the process layer; the metrics and accountability system: the organizational metric set (the compliance coverage trends showing audited, remediated, and conforming surface over time, the quality and velocity indicators of issue density and fix rates, the experience outcome measures from user testing beyond technical conformance, and the leading indicators of training completion, pattern adoption, and gate pass rates); the reporting architecture (executive dashboards with review cadence, team-level operational metrics inside sprint and release reporting, and external transparency through published accessibility statements); the target governance (accessibility objectives inside organizational planning, the metric validity reviews that prevent proxy gaming from section 12, and the improvement-supporting rather than punitive framing); and the maturity tracking (the periodic reassessment against the section 1 maturity model, industry benchmarking, and the investment analysis comparing the cost of building accessibly against the cost of exclusion and retrofit) as the accountability layer; the culture and advocacy layer: the empathy cultivation (disability awareness education that respects the limits of simulation from section 1, the centering of disabled user voices and stories over abstract compliance arguments, and inclusive design mindset training on the curb-cut principle); the community building (internal guilds and communities of practice, knowledge sharing rituals inside demos and retrospectives, and external participation in conferences, open source, and standards work from the ecosystem of section 13); the advocacy skills (bottom-up argumentation connecting user impact to business case for resistant contexts, top-down mandate integration, and coalition building across design, engineering, legal, and support functions); and the sustainability of practice (succession planning and burnout prevention so accessibility does not depend on individual heroes, documentation and training systems that survive staff turnover, and milestone recognition that maintains momentum) as the culture layer; the governance anti-patterns — the failure library: the policy without teeth (published commitment lacking gates, resources, or accountability, remedied by enforceable requirements and executive sponsorship); the ivory tower center of excellence (the central team disconnected from delivery reality, remedied by the hybrid embedded model); the design system bypass (teams circumventing verified components with custom inaccessible alternatives, remedied by bypass detection and contribution governance); the checkbox culture (gate-passing minimum effort without outcome focus, remedied by experience metrics and user testing); the hero dependence (single champions carrying the practice, remedied by distributed ownership and succession); the debt spiral (deferred accessibility work compounding remediation costs, remedied by shift-left and impact-weighted prioritization); the vendor blindness (third-party inaccessibility imported unexamined, remedied by procurement and dependency governance); and the metric theater (dashboard compliance without access improvement, remedied by validity review and benchmarking) with detection methods as the diagnostic discipline; and the governance deliverable — the policy, people, pattern, process, metrics, and culture system that scales accessible visualization practice across the organization.
