# Domain Content Map — Bertin's Semiology of Graphics: The Retinal Variables and the Levels of Reading

## Absorbed capabilities this module must produce
1. Apply Bertin's graphical semiology through the framework (graphic information system thesis with monosemy goal, three-questions organization, elementary sign anatomy and layer decomposition), master the retinal variables (complete inventory of position, size, value, hue, orientation, shape, and texture with property analysis across associative-dissociative-selective and quantitative-ordered-qualitative dimensions, capacity limits and length-of-variable concepts, interaction effects and construction of tables, Cleveland-McGill modern validation).
2. Operate the reading levels (elementary-intermediate-overall hierarchy, question-to-level mapping driving construction, layer decomposition and small multiples strategies, dashboard and interaction applications), extend across applications (thematic mapping systems, reorderable matrices as clustered heatmap ancestors, flow and movement graphics, legacy influence and known limitations).
3. Run the operational method (encoding decision procedure with data-type mapping and capacity checks, construction audits detecting order-by-hue and level mismatches, reexpression strategies, Bertin-plus-modern synthesis), and avoid order-by-hue, category-by-value, level mismatch, variable masking, legend dependency, fundamentalism, and aesthetic override failures.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the foundational system where semiotics becomes a rigorous design science for maps and charts: the graphic information system — Bertin's framework: the core thesis (the 1967 Semiology of Graphics argument that graphic expression constitutes a sign system governed by discoverable laws, making visualization design a science of visual communication rather than an art of taste; the monosemy goal where well-constructed graphics admit exactly one reading, the counterpart to section 1's polysemy problem; the three questions framework where any graphic answers what the components are, where they are located, and how they relate, organizing all graphic construction); the graphic sign anatomy (the image as the total graphic message, the graphic as a mark-bearing surface, and the elementary graphic sign as the smallest meaningful unit combining a visual variable with a spatial position; the figure-ground and visual-layer concepts where complex graphics decompose into readable layers; the construction rules where elementary signs combine under systematic principles rather than arbitrary arrangement) as the framework base; the retinal variables — the visual variable system: the complete variable inventory (position on the plane as the primary variable with unmatched precision and universality; size as the quantitative magnitude variable; value or lightness as the ordered intensity variable carrying the natural zero-to-maximum reading; color hue as the categorical discrimination variable with no inherent order; orientation as the directional variable; shape as the categorical form variable with limited capacity; and texture or grain as the density-pattern variable, each variable analyzed for its properties, capacity, and appropriate data types); the variable property analysis (the associative-dissociative-selective dimensions determining whether variables group, separate, or isolate information for different reading tasks; the quantitative-ordered-qualitative suitability of each variable for measurement, order, and category data respectively; the variable capacity limits, where hue distinguishes roughly seven categories while value supports fewer ordered steps, grounding the palette-capacity disciplines of the color traditions); the length-of-visual-variable concept (the range of discriminable steps within each variable, where position is effectively unlimited while shape is severely limited, driving encoding decisions for cardinality); the variable interaction effects (the combination and interference phenomena where simultaneous variables can reinforce, mask, or confuse each other; the construction-of-tables analysis where row-column arrangements of variables create systematic figure families; and the modern validation status where perception research has confirmed, refined, and occasionally corrected Bertin's original rankings, particularly the Cleveland-McGill elementary perceptual task hierarchy that reordered position, length, and angle precision) as the variable system; the levels of reading — the task hierarchy: the three reading levels (the elementary level reading individual sign values one at a time, the intermediate level comparing signs and identifying groups within a single layer, and the overall level perceiving global structure and relationships across the whole image; the design implication that graphics must support all three levels simultaneously, where a map readable point-by-point may fail at pattern level and vice versa); the reading-level-to-question mapping (the elementary level answering what-value questions about individual items, the intermediate answering comparison and grouping questions, and the overall answering structural and pattern questions; the question-first design method where the primary analytic question determines the required reading level and therefore the graphic construction); the layer and decomposition strategies (the multi-layer construction where different variables carry different information layers, each readable at its own level; the figure decomposition where complex questions split into series of simpler graphics each optimized for one reading level; and the small-multiples logic as systematic decomposition preserving comparison capability across panels); and the modern application (the reading levels in dashboard design where overview panels serve overall reading and detail panels serve elementary reading, connecting to information-architecture traditions; the level support in interactive systems where zoom and filter transitions move readers between levels deliberately) as the reading layer; the Bertin applications — maps, matrices, and diagrams: the cartographic applications (the thematic mapping system where retinal variables encode data on geographic position, the direct lineage to modern choropleth and proportional symbol maps; the map construction rules including the visual variable selection by data type and the legend as the decoding key, systems still governing map design; the surface and network cartography extensions from the semiology tradition); the matrix and table graphics (the table-network-diagram continuum where relational data finds graphic forms ordered by construction principle; the reorderable matrix concept where row-column sorting reveals block structure invisible in arbitrary order, the ancestor of modern clustered heatmaps and seriation methods; the graph and network layout as position-variable optimization problems); the flow and movement graphics (the stock-and-flow representations with their variable mappings; the movement-on-map conventions using arrows and trajectory lines with orientation and shape variables; the modern descendant systems from Sankey diagrams to animation-based flow visualization); and the legacy assessment (the influence traceable through Tufte's data-ink principles, the Cleveland perceptual experiments, the Mackinlay automated design theory of section 4, and the Munzner nested model's variable treatment; the known limitations including the static-print origin predating interaction and animation, the color science of its era superseded by modern appearance models, and the underdeveloped treatment of text and annotation as sign systems) as the applications layer; the operational Bertin method — the working discipline: the encoding decision procedure (the data-type-to-variable mapping as the first filter, where quantitative ordered data takes position, length, or value while categorical data takes hue or shape; the reading-level check asking whether the chosen encoding supports the primary question's required level; the capacity check verifying that data cardinality fits variable capacity limits; and the layer-conflict check ensuring simultaneous variables reinforce rather than mask the intended readings); the graphic construction audit (the systematic review of any visualization against variable properties, reading levels, and construction rules, extending the semiotic analysis method of section 1 with Bertin's rigor; the common failure detection including order carried by unordered variables, categories carried by ordered variables beyond discrimination capacity, and pattern questions answered with elementary-level-only encodings); the reexpression strategies (the transformation and reorganization options when initial constructions fail, from Bertin's systematic construction-of-tables exploration; the modern equivalents in view manipulation, aggregation, and faceting strategies from the information-architecture traditions); and the Bertin-plus-modern synthesis (the integration with perceptual science where Cleveland-McGill rankings refine variable precision ordering; the extension to interaction where reading levels map onto overview-detail navigation and variable capacity extends through dynamic queries; and the persistence of core principles where position primacy, capacity limits, and level support remain design law across seven decades of technological change) as the operational layer; and the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the order-by-hue error, encoding ordered data with unordered color hues so readers cannot rank values, remedied by the variable-property mapping discipline; the category-by-value confusion, distinguishing categories with lightness steps beyond discrimination capacity, remedied by the capacity-check procedure; the pattern-question-elementary-answer mismatch, asking readers to see global structure in encodings supporting only point reading, remedied by the reading-level-to-question mapping; the variable-masking overload, stacking simultaneous variables that interfere and destroy all intended readings, remedied by the layer-conflict audit; the legend-dependency design, building graphics requiring constant key consultation instead of direct or redundant encoding, remedied by the construction rules and labeling craft; the Bertin-fundamentalism, applying 1967 static-print rules dogmatically to interactive and animated contexts without extension, remedied by the modern-synthesis integration; and the aesthetic-override, letting style preferences defeat variable-selection logic, remedied by the decision-procedure discipline with detection methods as the diagnostic.
