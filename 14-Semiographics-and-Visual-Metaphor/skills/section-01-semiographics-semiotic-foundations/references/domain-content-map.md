## Absorbed capabilities this module must produce
1. Ground visual communication in semiotic foundations through the Saussurean sign model (signifier-signified pairing, arbitrariness explaining learned conventions, value-through-difference explaining palette discriminability, langue-parole and code concepts, syntagmatic-paradigmatic axes for layout and encoding analysis).
2. Apply the Peirce taxonomy (icon-index-symbol categories with degrees of iconicity, diagrammatic resemblance as the chart category, indexical evidential authority of data graphics, symbolic learning burdens, and mixed-sign decomposition skills), understand the visual sign specifically (analog-digital distinction driving encoding principles, polysemy and anchorage, perceptual foundations gating meaning, motivated-arbitrary tradeoffs), reason pragmatically about context (interpretants and co-created meaning, context dependence with frame and genre conventions, graphic literacy and expertise reversal, designer responsibility in encoding as meaning decisions).
3. Operate the semiotic analysis method (sign inventory protocols with layer analysis, connotation-denotation analysis, substitution-combination testing, misreading diagnosis, and honesty about analysis limits), and avoid natural-meaning fallacies, private symbols, context amnesia, polysemy neglect, jargon performance, and perceptual blindness failures.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the theoretical bedrock of meaning-making that the entire course builds upon: the sign as the unit of meaning — the Saussurean foundation: the dyadic sign model (the signifier and signified pairing from the structuralist tradition, where a mark or sound pattern connects to a mental concept; the arbitrariness of the sign, the principle that most connections between form and meaning are conventional rather than natural, explaining why visual languages must be learned; the value-through-difference principle, where signs gain meaning from their contrasts with other signs in the system rather than in isolation, a concept that directly explains why encoding palettes work through discriminability); the sign systems and codes (the langue and parole distinction between the underlying system and individual utterances, applied to visual languages where design systems function as langue and individual charts as parole; the code concept as the shared convention set enabling communication, and the encoding-decoding model of communication with its noise and ambiguity sources; the syntagmatic and paradigmatic axes, where syntagms are combinations present together in a display and paradigms are the substitutable alternatives, giving designers a vocabulary for both layout composition and encoding choice); and the semiotic triangle extension (the Ogden-Richards reference model connecting symbol, thought, and referent with the missing direct link, explaining miscommunication when designers and readers attach different referents to the same mark) as the Saussurean layer; the Peirce taxonomy — icon, index, symbol: the icon category (signs resembling their objects, from portraits to realistic pictograms, and the degrees-of-iconicity concept recognizing resemblance as a continuum rather than a binary; the image, diagram, and metaphor subtypes in Peirce's analysis, where diagrams resemble through relational structure rather than surface appearance, making them the key category for charts and graphs); the index category (signs connected by causation or contiguity, from smoke indicating fire to a thermometer column indicating temperature, and the indexical quality of data graphics where marks are causally produced by data values, the foundation of their evidential authority); the symbol category (signs connected purely by convention, including words, numerals, and most chart encodings such as color assignments in legends; the symbol-learning burden explaining why novel visual vocabularies require legends, onboarding, and repeated exposure); and the mixed-sign reality of data graphics (the typical chart as an icon-index-symbol composite where bar length is indexical, axis numerals are symbolic, and a tree-map rectangle is diagrammatically iconic; the analysis skill of decomposing any visualization into its sign types to diagnose comprehension problems) as the Peircean layer; the visual sign specifically — from language to image: the analog-versus-digital sign distinction (the continuous-variation character of visual signs versus the discrete-unit character of language, from the semiotics of image traditions; the implications for encoding, where continuous visual variables suit continuous data and discrete variables suit categorical data, a principle formalized later in Bertin's system of section 3); the visual meaning without grammar (the debate over whether images have grammar-like structure or operate through different principles, previewing the visual-grammar traditions of section 2; the openness and polysemy of visual signs, where images support multiple readings in ways language resists, and the anchorage function of text that pins visual meaning, a concept central to the Barthes analysis of section 2); the perceptual foundations of visual meaning (the pre-semantic layer where Gestalt organization and preattentive processing create structure before interpretation, connecting to the perception courses; the double articulation question, whether visual communication has language-like levels of meaningless units combining into meaningful ones, with the retinal variables of section 3 as the closest candidate); and the motivated versus arbitrary spectrum in graphics (natural mappings where form suggests meaning, such as up-is-more, versus arbitrary conventions such as red-for-category-A; the motivated-sign advantages in learnability and the arbitrary-sign advantages in capacity and precision, establishing the core tradeoff of visual language design) as the visual layer; meaning in context — the pragmatic turn: the interpretant and unlimited semiosis (the Peircean concept of meaning as an ongoing interpretive process rather than a fixed transfer, where each reading generates new interpretive signs; the practical consequence that visualization meaning is co-created by designers and readers rather than transmitted intact); the context dependence of meaning (the same sign carrying different meanings across contexts, from red as danger to red as celebration to red as negative financial values; the frame and genre conventions that set interpretive expectations, where a financial chart, a scientific figure, and a political infographic trigger different reading contracts); the reader competence and literacy (the graphic literacy concept, where chart reading is a learned cultural skill with historical development, explaining generational and educational differences in comprehension; the expertise reversal in sign interpretation, where conventions transparent to experts become opaque to novices, connecting to the cognitive-load traditions); and the designer responsibility in meaning-making (the encoding decisions as meaning decisions, where every visual choice constrains and enables interpretation; the ambiguity management craft of designing signs whose range of readings includes the intended one) as the pragmatic layer; the semiotic analysis method — the working tool: the sign inventory protocol (the systematic decomposition of a visualization into its constituent signs, cataloging each mark's signifier, signified, and sign type; the layer analysis separating data marks, structural elements, annotations, and decorations as distinct sign systems with different functions); the connotation and denotation analysis (the denotative reading of what a sign literally indicates versus its connotative associations, such as a jagged line denoting volatility and connoting danger or excitement; the ideological connotation awareness where visual styles carry values and worldviews, previewing the rhetorical analysis of section 11); the substitution and combination testing (the paradigmatic experiment of asking what each sign could be replaced with and how meaning would shift; the syntagmatic analysis of how sign combinations create meanings none carries alone, such as color plus position plus size interactions in a bubble chart); the misreading diagnosis application (the systematic tracing of comprehension failures to specific sign problems including ambiguous signifiers, unlearned conventions, conflicting contexts, and connotation interference; the semiotic audit as a design review method, previewing the design-system applications of section 14); and the analysis limitations honesty (the over-intellectualization risk where semiotic vocabulary describes rather than improves practice; the empirical complement where perception research and user testing ground semiotic hypotheses, a discipline maintained throughout the course) as the method layer; and the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the natural-meaning fallacy, assuming visual signs carry inherent meanings readers automatically extract, remedied by the arbitrariness and learned-convention literacy; the private symbol problem, inventing sign systems only the designer can decode, remedied by the convention and competence analysis; the context amnesia, reusing signs across contexts where their meanings shift dangerously, remedied by the pragmatic context discipline; the polysemy neglect, shipping ambiguous visuals without anchorage, remedied by the meaning-range management craft; the jargon performance, citing signifier and signified in reviews while producing no better encodings, remedied by the analysis-method application requirement; and the perceptual blindness, theorizing about meaning while ignoring the pre-semantic visual system that gates all interpretation, remedied by the perception foundations integration with detection methods as the diagnostic.
