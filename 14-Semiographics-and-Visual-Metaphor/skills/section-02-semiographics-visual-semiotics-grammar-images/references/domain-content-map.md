# Domain Content Map — Visual Semiotics and the Grammar of Images: From Barthes to Visual Design Grammar

Read this during Phase 3 (architecture) and Phase 4 (writing) of the module
development workflow. Verify all factual claims and numbers against the
authoritative sources before publishing (Phase 2 evidence base); flag
anything disputed rather than repeating it.

## Absorbed capabilities this module must produce
1. Analyze and design image meaning through the Barthes tradition (three message levels, anchorage fixing polysemy, relay complementarity, connotation systems and myth critique, application to chart registers and anchorage hierarchies), wield the Kress-van Leeuwen visual grammar (ideational-interpersonal-textual metafunctions, narrative-conceptual representations, contact-distance-perspective interactive systems, information-value-salience-framing compositional systems, critical reading application).
2. Build with modern operational grammars (grammar of graphics traditions in declarative visualization languages.
3. Design systems as visual langue with semantic discipline, layout grammars and pattern languages), extend to temporal semiotics (motion semantics, interaction affordance signifiers and feedback signs, state-transition mapping, multimodal anchorage).
4. Assess honestly (grammar analogy limits requiring empirical grounding, cultural-historical variability, lens-not-law integration with semiotics-perception-testing triangulation, analysis-to-synthesis literacy), and avoid unanchored images, connotation accidents, grammar dogmatism, reading-direction universalism, interaction neglect, and theory-practice divorce failures.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the traditions analyzing how images produce meaning and how visual compositions can be read with language-like rigor: the Barthes analysis — rhetoric of the image: the three message levels (the linguistic message of captions and labels accompanying images, the coded iconic message of culturally learned connotations, and the non-coded iconic message of literal denotative recognition, from the classic rhetoric-of-the-image analysis; the anchorage function where text fixes the floating chain of signifieds in an image, stopping polysemy by selecting one reading and ignoring others, the mechanism behind every chart title, axis label, and annotation; the relay function where text and image stand in complementary relation, each carrying part of the meaning, the structure of scrollytelling and annotated graphics); the connotation systems in images (the stylistic, chromatic, and compositional connotations carrying cultural meanings beyond denoted objects, from photographic traditions applied to data graphics; the myth concept where connoted meanings naturalize cultural values, presenting constructed readings as obvious facts, the critical tool for analyzing persuasive infographics previewed for section 11); and the application to data visualization (the chart-as-image analysis where data graphics carry connotative registers through style choices, from playful to authoritative to alarming; the anchorage hierarchy in charts where titles anchor the whole, axis labels anchor scales, and annotations anchor specific marks; the denotation-connotation balance craft where scientific figures suppress connotation while editorial graphics exploit it deliberately) as the Barthes layer; the visual grammar tradition — Kress and van Leeuwen: the grammar of visual design framework (the claim that visual compositions organize meaning through regular, learnable systems analogous to grammar, from the Reading Images tradition; the three metafunctions: ideational meaning about what is represented, interpersonal meaning about relations with viewers, and textual meaning about composition and information flow); the representational systems (narrative representations showing participants in action through vectors and directional elements versus conceptual representations classifying and analyzing through symbolic attribution and taxonomic arrangement; the participant and circumstance vocabulary for analyzing who and what appears in an image and under what conditions; the application to charts where trend lines create narrative vectors and legend taxonomies create conceptual classification); the interactive systems (the contact dimension where offered information versus demanded attention distinguishes presentation modes; the social distance implied by framing and crop, from intimate close-up to impersonal long shot, applying to data zoom levels where cell-level detail feels intimate and national aggregates feel distant; the perspective and involvement dimensions where frontal angles signal involvement and oblique angles signal detachment, and vertical perspective encodes power relations, mapping onto the design choices of dashboard hierarchy and emphasis); the compositional systems (the information value of placement where left-given and right-new, top-ideal and bottom-real structure reading expectations, directly applicable to dashboard layout and chart composition; the salience systems where size, contrast, color, and foregrounding create attention hierarchies connecting to the preattentive traditions; the framing systems where connecting and separating devices signal belonging and difference, explaining whitespace, borders, and grouping in chart design); and the critical-reading application (the grammar analysis as design review method, examining what a composition's placements, distances, and framings say beyond its data; the ideological reading where compositional choices reveal and construct power relations and values, previewing section 11's rhetorical analysis) as the grammar layer; the modern visual design grammars — operational descendants: the visualization design grammars (the grammar-of-graphics tradition in the Wickham and Wilkinson lineage where charts are built by composing data mappings, geometric elements, scales, and coordinates through formal rules; the declarative visualization languages as implemented grammars, from Vega-Lite specifications to Observable Plot, where syntax encodes design decisions systematically); the design systems as visual languages (the component-token-pattern hierarchies functioning as langue for product visualization, connecting to section 14's system building; the semantic naming and consistent usage rules as the code maintenance discipline; the extension mechanisms where novel charts must integrate with established grammar rather than break it); the layout grammars (the grid systems and spacing scales as syntactic rules governing element combination; the visual hierarchy conventions as information-value implementations; the responsive transformation rules as grammar-preserving adaptation across contexts); and the pattern languages (the Alexander lineage of pattern languages applied to interface and visualization design, where each pattern documents a problem, solution, and forces in combinable form; the pattern grammar composition where combinations create meanings beyond individual patterns, from section 1's syntagmatic principle) as the operational layer; the moving and interactive image — temporal semiotics: the time-based visual meaning (the motion and transition semantics where animation direction, speed, and easing carry meanings such as causality, hierarchy, and relationship, connecting to the narrative-course motion craft; the temporal anchorage where timelines, progress indicators, and state persistence orient viewers in moving visualizations); the interaction semiotics (the affordance and signifier distinction from the Norman tradition where perceived properties signal possible actions, and visual signifiers communicate affordances; the feedback signs where system responses function as indexical signs of state change; the interaction vocabularies as learned codes where drag, click, and swipe conventions vary across platforms and must be signaled); the state and transition meaning (the visual state changes as signs of data and system states, requiring consistent mapping from the dynamic-content traditions; the transition continuity as indexical sign preserving object identity through change, connecting to animation object permanence); and the multimodal composition (the image-text-motion-sound integration where each mode anchors and extends the others, from the multimedia learning traditions; the channel redundancy and complementarity decisions as semiotic design choices about which meanings travel by which modes) as the temporal layer; the critique and limits of visual grammar — the honest assessment: the analogy limits (the grammar metaphor's productive power for systematic analysis versus its overreach where image meaning resists language-like formalization, a debate running through the visual-semiotics literature; the empirical grounding question where grammar claims require validation through comprehension testing rather than theoretical elegance alone); the cultural and historical variability (the grammar conventions varying across cultures and eras, where left-right information values assume left-to-right reading cultures, previewing section 10's cross-cultural analysis; the convention evolution where today's natural mappings were yesterday's learned novelties, as chart history demonstrates); the practice integration balance (the grammar as lens not law, a reading and design vocabulary enriching critique without constraining invention; the semiotics-plus-perception-plus-testing triangulation where theoretical analysis, perceptual science, and empirical validation each correct the others' blind spots); and the working literacy goal (the fluent designer reading any visual composition for its representational, interactive, and compositional meanings while constructing compositions with deliberate grammatical choices; the analysis-to-synthesis bridge where deconstructive reading skills feed constructive design capability) as the critique layer; and the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the unanchored image, shipping ambiguous visuals without titles, labels, or context to fix meaning, remedied by the anchorage hierarchy discipline; the connotation accident, letting style choices carry unintended registers of alarm, playfulness, or authority, remedied by the connotation analysis craft; the grammar dogmatism, applying compositional rules mechanically without purpose or audience fit, remedied by the grammar-as-lens-not-law positioning; the reading-direction universalism, assuming left-given-right-new structures work for all audiences, remedied by the cultural variability awareness of section 10; the interaction-semiotics neglect, leaving affordances unsigned so users cannot perceive possible actions, remedied by the signifier and feedback design discipline; and the theory-practice divorce, mastering analytical vocabulary without improved design output, remedied by the analysis-to-synthesis bridge requirement with detection methods as the diagnostic.
