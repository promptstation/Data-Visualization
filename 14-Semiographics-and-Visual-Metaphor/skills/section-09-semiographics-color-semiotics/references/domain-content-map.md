# Domain Content Map — Color Semiotics: Cultural, Contextual, and Systematic Meaning of Color

Read this during Phase 3 (architecture) and Phase 4 (writing) of the module
development workflow. Verify all factual claims and numbers against the
authoritative sources before publishing (Phase 2 evidence base); flag
anything disputed rather than repeating it.

## Absorbed capabilities this module must produce
1. Command color as a sign system through foundations (perception-convention duality with biological constraints, association research on consensus-divergence-context, harmony and contrast systems with relational meaning, proportionate color emotion literacy), navigate cultural lexicons (red-green-blue spectrum meanings across danger-finance-celebration-trust-mourning contexts, remaining hue conventions including the white-black polarity reversal, audience audit and domain precedence discipline with intra-graphic consistency).
2. Build functional color systems (semantic status-diverging-sequential schemes with accessibility requirements, categorical discipline with symbol learning burdens and capacity limits, emphasis-de-emphasis systems, channel interaction with redundancy and layering architecture, token governance with theme verification), wield color rhetoric (register-tone matching with genre connotations, emotional palette arcs and accent strategies, ideological-ethical audits covering political-gender-racial-cause dimensions, meaning documentation with connotation review checkpoints), and avoid universal-meaning assumptions, convention violations, status monochrome, rainbow defaults, emphasis inflation, unexamined identity coding, palette drift, and undocumented rationale failures.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers color as a sign system where perception science, cultural convention, and contextual framing jointly determine meaning: the color sign foundations — perception meeting convention: the dual nature of color meaning (the perceptual layer where color discrimination, contrast, and harmony operate through human visual biology largely shared across populations, from the color science traditions; the conventional layer where specific hues carry learned cultural meanings that vary across societies and eras, the semiotic analysis of section 1 applied to the most emotionally charged visual variable; the interaction reality where biology constrains what conventions can achieve, since no cultural meaning survives undiscriminable color assignments, connecting to the CVD and contrast disciplines of the accessibility traditions); the color association research (the cross-cultural consensus findings where warm-cool, active-passive, and weight associations show partial universality likely grounded in shared environment experience, from the color psychology literature; the divergence findings where specific hue meanings vary dramatically by culture, the raw material for section 10's cross-cultural analysis; the context-dependence evidence where the same color reads differently in finance, medicine, nature, and religion contexts, establishing context as the dominant meaning determinant); the color harmony and contrast systems (the historical harmony theories from the Itten and Albers traditions where simultaneous contrast demonstrates that color meaning and appearance shift with surrounding colors, the relational principle of section 1's value-through-difference made visible; the modern appearance models accounting for adaptation and surround effects from the color science traditions; the harmony connotations where different color relationship types carry registers from vibrant tension through calm unity, resources for deliberate connotative design); and the color emotion literature (the arousal and valence findings where saturated warm colors elevate activation while desaturated cool colors reduce it, with effect sizes modest but consistent enough for design leverage; the color mood mapping traditions in marketing and environmental design at practitioner awareness; the individual variation and the limits of color emotion determinism, keeping claims proportionate to evidence) as the foundation layer; the cultural color lexicons — meanings across contexts: the red spectrum (danger, warning, and prohibition in safety-signage systems with international standardization; loss, debt, and decline in Western financial convention where red ink means negative values; celebration, luck, and prosperity in Chinese and broader East Asian contexts where red dominates festival and wedding visual culture; martyrdom and sacrifice in Christian iconography; and revolution and political left association from historical flag traditions, making red the most context-sensitive hue in data visualization); the green spectrum (nature, environment, and sustainability associations from the ecological movements; safety, go, and positive status from traffic-convention inheritance in dashboard semantics; profit and gain in financial contexts as red's counterpart; money association through currency conventions specifically in United States contexts; and religious significance in Islamic traditions with careful usage implications for international audiences); the blue spectrum (trust, stability, and corporate professionalism from decades of institutional branding making blue the default authority color; calm and coolness from environmental association with sky and water; sadness in the English-idiom melancholy sense, a linguistic association not universally shared; political association varying by country where blue indicates conservative parties in some nations and other positions elsewhere; and the gender-association history where pink-blue assignments reversed during the twentieth century, the canonical demonstration of color convention contingency); the remaining hue conventions (yellow's caution-warning association from visibility conventions and its optimism-warmth readings in other contexts; orange's energy-creativity registers with safety-visibility functions; purple's royalty-spirituality-mystery associations from historical dye scarcity; white-purity-cleanliness in Western contexts versus mourning-death in several East Asian traditions, the most consequential polarity reversal for international design; black's sophistication-formality versus mourning-death-doom ranges; and brown-earth-tone natural-organic-rustic registers); and the lexicon application discipline (the audience color audit checking each planned color assignment against the cultural lexicons of the actual readership; the domain convention precedence where finance, medical, and safety contexts carry strong inherited color semantics that override aesthetic preference; and the intra-graphic consistency principle where colors maintain stable meaning within and across a product's visuals, from the coherence traditions) as the lexicon layer; the functional color systems — color at work in data graphics: the semantic color categories (the status color systems of success-warning-error-information conventions inherited from interface design, with their traffic-light entailments and the colorblind-accessibility requirements from the redundant encoding traditions; the diverging scheme semantics where two-hue oppositions with neutral centers carry good-bad, hot-cold, or agree-disagree mappings whose direction choice is a meaning decision; the sequential scheme semantics where light-to-dark progression imports magnitude, depth, and intensity readings with the perceptual uniformity requirements of the color science traditions); the categorical color discipline (the qualitative palette conventions where hue assignments to categories are arbitrary symbols requiring legends or direct labels, from section 1's symbol learning burden; the brand-color integration challenges where corporate palettes rarely provide sufficient discriminable categories, forcing systematic extension strategies; the color-count limits where categorical discrimination degrades beyond seven-ish hues per the capacity research of section 4, driving faceting and interaction alternatives); the emphasis and de-emphasis systems (the gray-context-with-color-focus pattern applying salience principles from the preattentive traditions; the highlight consistency obligations where emphasis colors maintain meaning across a story or product; the dimming semantics where reduced saturation imports background, inactive, and lesser-importance readings from section 7 of the narrative traditions); the color interaction with other channels (the redundancy strategy pairing color with shape, position, or texture for accessibility and robustness from the redundant encoding discipline; the interference risks where color groupings contradict spatial groupings, creating the variable masking failures of section 3; the layered color architecture where base data colors, annotation colors, and interface colors occupy distinct gamuts preventing semantic collision); and the systematic color governance (the color token systems distributing verified palettes through design systems, connecting to section 14; the palette generation tooling from the scientific colormap and accessible palette traditions; the dark mode and theme variants requiring complete semantic re-verification per the accessibility traditions) as the functional layer; the color connotation in composition — the rhetorical layer: the color register and tone (the saturation-brightness combinations establishing registers from muted-editorial through vibrant-consumer to neon-alarming, applying section 2's connotation analysis to palette-level choices; the period and genre connotations where specific palettes signal eras, movements, and media genres, from sepia-archival through vaporwave-digital aesthetics; the audience-appropriateness matching where scientific, journalistic, corporate, and consumer contexts carry palette expectations whose violation reads as incompetence or as deliberate subversion); the emotional narrative arc of color (the palette progression across a story or report where color temperature and saturation shifts mark narrative movement, connecting to the narrative course traditions; the single-color stories where monochromatic systems build hierarchy through value and saturation alone, requiring exceptional discipline; the accent strategy where one saturated hue against restrained grounds carries all emphasis weight, the typographic hero-number logic of section 8 in color); the ideological and ethical color dimensions (the political color mappings carrying partisan meanings that can silently endorse or antagonize, requiring deliberate audit in public-interest graphics; the gender, race, and identity color coding history where skin-tone palettes, gendered pink-blue binaries, and national color stereotypes carry representational harm risks, demanding the inclusive design review of section 10; the greenwashing and cause-color exploitation where environmental and social cause palettes lend unearned credibility, previewing section 11's rhetorical ethics); and the color meaning documentation (the palette rationale records explaining every color assignment's semantic basis for teams and future maintainers; the connotation review checkpoints where palette choices face cultural and contextual audit before production; the color accessibility verification where contrast, CVD safety, and redundancy checks gate release per the accessibility traditions) as the rhetorical layer; and the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the universal-meaning assumption, applying one culture's color semantics to global audiences without audit, remedied by the audience color audit discipline; the finance-convention violation, inverting red-green profit-loss mappings within contexts that inherit them, breaking instant comprehension, remedied by the domain convention precedence; the status-color monochrome, encoding success-error states in color alone excluding colorblind users, remedied by the redundancy obligations; the rainbow categorical default, applying spectral palettes to unordered categories importing false order and exhausting discriminability, remedied by the qualitative palette discipline; the emphasis inflation, highlighting everything until emphasis means nothing, remedied by the accent strategy scarcity principle; the unexamined identity coding, deploying gendered or racialized color conventions without representational review, remedied by the inclusive design audit; the palette drift, colors accumulating inconsistent meanings across a product's surfaces, remedied by the token governance and consistency principles; and the undocumented rationale, palettes whose meanings live only in departed designers' heads, remedied by the documentation obligations with detection methods as the diagnostic.
