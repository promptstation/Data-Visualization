# Curriculum Content Outlook — Semiographics & Visual Metaphor

**Section 1 — Semiotic Foundations: Signs, Meaning, and the Structure of Visual Communication**

**Summary:** Covers the theoretical bedrock of meaning-making that the entire course builds upon: the sign as the unit of meaning — the Saussurean foundation: the dyadic sign model (the signifier and signified pairing from the structuralist tradition, where a mark or sound pattern connects to a mental concept; the arbitrariness of the sign, the principle that most connections between form and meaning are conventional rather than natural, explaining why visual languages must be learned; the value-through-difference principle, where signs gain meaning from their contrasts with other signs in the system rather than in isolation, a concept that directly explains why encoding palettes work through discriminability); the sign systems and codes (the langue and parole distinction between the underlying system and individual utterances, applied to visual languages where design systems function as langue and individual charts as parole; the code concept as the shared convention set enabling communication, and the encoding-decoding model of communication with its noise and ambiguity sources; the syntagmatic and paradigmatic axes, where syntagms are combinations present together in a display and paradigms are the substitutable alternatives, giving designers a vocabulary for both layout composition and encoding choice); and the semiotic triangle extension (the Ogden-Richards reference model connecting symbol, thought, and referent with the missing direct link, explaining miscommunication when designers and readers attach different referents to the same mark) as the Saussurean layer; the Peirce taxonomy — icon, index, symbol: the icon category (signs resembling their objects, from portraits to realistic pictograms, and the degrees-of-iconicity concept recognizing resemblance as a continuum rather than a binary; the image, diagram, and metaphor subtypes in Peirce's analysis, where diagrams resemble through relational structure rather than surface appearance, making them the key category for charts and graphs); the index category (signs connected by causation or contiguity, from smoke indicating fire to a thermometer column indicating temperature, and the indexical quality of data graphics where marks are causally produced by data values, the foundation of their evidential authority); the symbol category (signs connected purely by convention, including words, numerals, and most chart encodings such as color assignments in legends; the symbol-learning burden explaining why novel visual vocabularies require legends, onboarding, and repeated exposure); and the mixed-sign reality of data graphics (the typical chart as an icon-index-symbol composite where bar length is indexical, axis numerals are symbolic, and a tree-map rectangle is diagrammatically iconic; the analysis skill of decomposing any visualization into its sign types to diagnose comprehension problems) as the Peircean layer; the visual sign specifically — from language to image: the analog-versus-digital sign distinction (the continuous-variation character of visual signs versus the discrete-unit character of language, from the semiotics of image traditions; the implications for encoding, where continuous visual variables suit continuous data and discrete variables suit categorical data, a principle formalized later in Bertin's system of section 3); the visual meaning without grammar (the debate over whether images have grammar-like structure or operate through different principles, previewing the visual-grammar traditions of section 2; the openness and polysemy of visual signs, where images support multiple readings in ways language resists, and the anchorage function of text that pins visual meaning, a concept central to the Barthes analysis of section 2); the perceptual foundations of visual meaning (the pre-semantic layer where Gestalt organization and preattentive processing create structure before interpretation, connecting to the perception courses; the double articulation question, whether visual communication has language-like levels of meaningless units combining into meaningful ones, with the retinal variables of section 3 as the closest candidate); and the motivated versus arbitrary spectrum in graphics (natural mappings where form suggests meaning, such as up-is-more, versus arbitrary conventions such as red-for-category-A; the motivated-sign advantages in learnability and the arbitrary-sign advantages in capacity and precision, establishing the core tradeoff of visual language design) as the visual layer; meaning in context — the pragmatic turn: the interpretant and unlimited semiosis (the Peircean concept of meaning as an ongoing interpretive process rather than a fixed transfer, where each reading generates new interpretive signs; the practical consequence that visualization meaning is co-created by designers and readers rather than transmitted intact); the context dependence of meaning (the same sign carrying different meanings across contexts, from red as danger to red as celebration to red as negative financial values; the frame and genre conventions that set interpretive expectations, where a financial chart, a scientific figure, and a political infographic trigger different reading contracts); the reader competence and literacy (the graphic literacy concept, where chart reading is a learned cultural skill with historical development, explaining generational and educational differences in comprehension; the expertise reversal in sign interpretation, where conventions transparent to experts become opaque to novices, connecting to the cognitive-load traditions); and the designer responsibility in meaning-making (the encoding decisions as meaning decisions, where every visual choice constrains and enables interpretation; the ambiguity management craft of designing signs whose range of readings includes the intended one) as the pragmatic layer; the semiotic analysis method — the working tool: the sign inventory protocol (the systematic decomposition of a visualization into its constituent signs, cataloging each mark's signifier, signified, and sign type; the layer analysis separating data marks, structural elements, annotations, and decorations as distinct sign systems with different functions); the connotation and denotation analysis (the denotative reading of what a sign literally indicates versus its connotative associations, such as a jagged line denoting volatility and connoting danger or excitement; the ideological connotation awareness where visual styles carry values and worldviews, previewing the rhetorical analysis of section 11); the substitution and combination testing (the paradigmatic experiment of asking what each sign could be replaced with and how meaning would shift; the syntagmatic analysis of how sign combinations create meanings none carries alone, such as color plus position plus size interactions in a bubble chart); the misreading diagnosis application (the systematic tracing of comprehension failures to specific sign problems including ambiguous signifiers, unlearned conventions, conflicting contexts, and connotation interference; the semiotic audit as a design review method, previewing the design-system applications of section 14); and the analysis limitations honesty (the over-intellectualization risk where semiotic vocabulary describes rather than improves practice; the empirical complement where perception research and user testing ground semiotic hypotheses, a discipline maintained throughout the course) as the method layer; and the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the natural-meaning fallacy, assuming visual signs carry inherent meanings readers automatically extract, remedied by the arbitrariness and learned-convention literacy; the private symbol problem, inventing sign systems only the designer can decode, remedied by the convention and competence analysis; the context amnesia, reusing signs across contexts where their meanings shift dangerously, remedied by the pragmatic context discipline; the polysemy neglect, shipping ambiguous visuals without anchorage, remedied by the meaning-range management craft; the jargon performance, citing signifier and signified in reviews while producing no better encodings, remedied by the analysis-method application requirement; and the perceptual blindness, theorizing about meaning while ignoring the pre-semantic visual system that gates all interpretation, remedied by the perception foundations integration with detection methods as the diagnostic.

**Absorbed Skill:** Ground visual communication in semiotic foundations through the Saussurean sign model (signifier-signified pairing, arbitrariness explaining learned conventions, value-through-difference explaining palette discriminability, langue-parole and code concepts, syntagmatic-paradigmatic axes for layout and encoding analysis), apply the Peirce taxonomy (icon-index-symbol categories with degrees of iconicity, diagrammatic resemblance as the chart category, indexical evidential authority of data graphics, symbolic learning burdens, and mixed-sign decomposition skills), understand the visual sign specifically (analog-digital distinction driving encoding principles, polysemy and anchorage, perceptual foundations gating meaning, motivated-arbitrary tradeoffs), reason pragmatically about context (interpretants and co-created meaning, context dependence with frame and genre conventions, graphic literacy and expertise reversal, designer responsibility in encoding as meaning decisions), operate the semiotic analysis method (sign inventory protocols with layer analysis, connotation-denotation analysis, substitution-combination testing, misreading diagnosis, and honesty about analysis limits), and avoid natural-meaning fallacies, private symbols, context amnesia, polysemy neglect, jargon performance, and perceptual blindness failures.

**Section 2 — Visual Semiotics and the Grammar of Images: From Barthes to Visual Design Grammar**

**Summary:** Covers the traditions analyzing how images produce meaning and how visual compositions can be read with language-like rigor: the Barthes analysis — rhetoric of the image: the three message levels (the linguistic message of captions and labels accompanying images, the coded iconic message of culturally learned connotations, and the non-coded iconic message of literal denotative recognition, from the classic rhetoric-of-the-image analysis; the anchorage function where text fixes the floating chain of signifieds in an image, stopping polysemy by selecting one reading and ignoring others, the mechanism behind every chart title, axis label, and annotation; the relay function where text and image stand in complementary relation, each carrying part of the meaning, the structure of scrollytelling and annotated graphics); the connotation systems in images (the stylistic, chromatic, and compositional connotations carrying cultural meanings beyond denoted objects, from photographic traditions applied to data graphics; the myth concept where connoted meanings naturalize cultural values, presenting constructed readings as obvious facts, the critical tool for analyzing persuasive infographics previewed for section 11); and the application to data visualization (the chart-as-image analysis where data graphics carry connotative registers through style choices, from playful to authoritative to alarming; the anchorage hierarchy in charts where titles anchor the whole, axis labels anchor scales, and annotations anchor specific marks; the denotation-connotation balance craft where scientific figures suppress connotation while editorial graphics exploit it deliberately) as the Barthes layer; the visual grammar tradition — Kress and van Leeuwen: the grammar of visual design framework (the claim that visual compositions organize meaning through regular, learnable systems analogous to grammar, from the Reading Images tradition; the three metafunctions: ideational meaning about what is represented, interpersonal meaning about relations with viewers, and textual meaning about composition and information flow); the representational systems (narrative representations showing participants in action through vectors and directional elements versus conceptual representations classifying and analyzing through symbolic attribution and taxonomic arrangement; the participant and circumstance vocabulary for analyzing who and what appears in an image and under what conditions; the application to charts where trend lines create narrative vectors and legend taxonomies create conceptual classification); the interactive systems (the contact dimension where offered information versus demanded attention distinguishes presentation modes; the social distance implied by framing and crop, from intimate close-up to impersonal long shot, applying to data zoom levels where cell-level detail feels intimate and national aggregates feel distant; the perspective and involvement dimensions where frontal angles signal involvement and oblique angles signal detachment, and vertical perspective encodes power relations, mapping onto the design choices of dashboard hierarchy and emphasis); the compositional systems (the information value of placement where left-given and right-new, top-ideal and bottom-real structure reading expectations, directly applicable to dashboard layout and chart composition; the salience systems where size, contrast, color, and foregrounding create attention hierarchies connecting to the preattentive traditions; the framing systems where connecting and separating devices signal belonging and difference, explaining whitespace, borders, and grouping in chart design); and the critical-reading application (the grammar analysis as design review method, examining what a composition's placements, distances, and framings say beyond its data; the ideological reading where compositional choices reveal and construct power relations and values, previewing section 11's rhetorical analysis) as the grammar layer; the modern visual design grammars — operational descendants: the visualization design grammars (the grammar-of-graphics tradition in the Wickham and Wilkinson lineage where charts are built by composing data mappings, geometric elements, scales, and coordinates through formal rules; the declarative visualization languages as implemented grammars, from Vega-Lite specifications to Observable Plot, where syntax encodes design decisions systematically); the design systems as visual languages (the component-token-pattern hierarchies functioning as langue for product visualization, connecting to section 14's system building; the semantic naming and consistent usage rules as the code maintenance discipline; the extension mechanisms where novel charts must integrate with established grammar rather than break it); the layout grammars (the grid systems and spacing scales as syntactic rules governing element combination; the visual hierarchy conventions as information-value implementations; the responsive transformation rules as grammar-preserving adaptation across contexts); and the pattern languages (the Alexander lineage of pattern languages applied to interface and visualization design, where each pattern documents a problem, solution, and forces in combinable form; the pattern grammar composition where combinations create meanings beyond individual patterns, from section 1's syntagmatic principle) as the operational layer; the moving and interactive image — temporal semiotics: the time-based visual meaning (the motion and transition semantics where animation direction, speed, and easing carry meanings such as causality, hierarchy, and relationship, connecting to the narrative-course motion craft; the temporal anchorage where timelines, progress indicators, and state persistence orient viewers in moving visualizations); the interaction semiotics (the affordance and signifier distinction from the Norman tradition where perceived properties signal possible actions, and visual signifiers communicate affordances; the feedback signs where system responses function as indexical signs of state change; the interaction vocabularies as learned codes where drag, click, and swipe conventions vary across platforms and must be signaled); the state and transition meaning (the visual state changes as signs of data and system states, requiring consistent mapping from the dynamic-content traditions; the transition continuity as indexical sign preserving object identity through change, connecting to animation object permanence); and the multimodal composition (the image-text-motion-sound integration where each mode anchors and extends the others, from the multimedia learning traditions; the channel redundancy and complementarity decisions as semiotic design choices about which meanings travel by which modes) as the temporal layer; the critique and limits of visual grammar — the honest assessment: the analogy limits (the grammar metaphor's productive power for systematic analysis versus its overreach where image meaning resists language-like formalization, a debate running through the visual-semiotics literature; the empirical grounding question where grammar claims require validation through comprehension testing rather than theoretical elegance alone); the cultural and historical variability (the grammar conventions varying across cultures and eras, where left-right information values assume left-to-right reading cultures, previewing section 10's cross-cultural analysis; the convention evolution where today's natural mappings were yesterday's learned novelties, as chart history demonstrates); the practice integration balance (the grammar as lens not law, a reading and design vocabulary enriching critique without constraining invention; the semiotics-plus-perception-plus-testing triangulation where theoretical analysis, perceptual science, and empirical validation each correct the others' blind spots); and the working literacy goal (the fluent designer reading any visual composition for its representational, interactive, and compositional meanings while constructing compositions with deliberate grammatical choices; the analysis-to-synthesis bridge where deconstructive reading skills feed constructive design capability) as the critique layer; and the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the unanchored image, shipping ambiguous visuals without titles, labels, or context to fix meaning, remedied by the anchorage hierarchy discipline; the connotation accident, letting style choices carry unintended registers of alarm, playfulness, or authority, remedied by the connotation analysis craft; the grammar dogmatism, applying compositional rules mechanically without purpose or audience fit, remedied by the grammar-as-lens-not-law positioning; the reading-direction universalism, assuming left-given-right-new structures work for all audiences, remedied by the cultural variability awareness of section 10; the interaction-semiotics neglect, leaving affordances unsigned so users cannot perceive possible actions, remedied by the signifier and feedback design discipline; and the theory-practice divorce, mastering analytical vocabulary without improved design output, remedied by the analysis-to-synthesis bridge requirement with detection methods as the diagnostic.

**Absorbed Skill:** Analyze and design image meaning through the Barthes tradition (three message levels, anchorage fixing polysemy, relay complementarity, connotation systems and myth critique, application to chart registers and anchorage hierarchies), wield the Kress-van Leeuwen visual grammar (ideational-interpersonal-textual metafunctions, narrative-conceptual representations, contact-distance-perspective interactive systems, information-value-salience-framing compositional systems, critical reading application), build with modern operational grammars (grammar of graphics traditions in declarative visualization languages, design systems as visual langue with semantic discipline, layout grammars and pattern languages), extend to temporal semiotics (motion semantics, interaction affordance signifiers and feedback signs, state-transition mapping, multimodal anchorage), assess honestly (grammar analogy limits requiring empirical grounding, cultural-historical variability, lens-not-law integration with semiotics-perception-testing triangulation, analysis-to-synthesis literacy), and avoid unanchored images, connotation accidents, grammar dogmatism, reading-direction universalism, interaction neglect, and theory-practice divorce failures.

**Section 3 — Bertin's Semiology of Graphics: The Retinal Variables and the Levels of Reading**

**Summary:** Covers the foundational system where semiotics becomes a rigorous design science for maps and charts: the graphic information system — Bertin's framework: the core thesis (the 1967 Semiology of Graphics argument that graphic expression constitutes a sign system governed by discoverable laws, making visualization design a science of visual communication rather than an art of taste; the monosemy goal where well-constructed graphics admit exactly one reading, the counterpart to section 1's polysemy problem; the three questions framework where any graphic answers what the components are, where they are located, and how they relate, organizing all graphic construction); the graphic sign anatomy (the image as the total graphic message, the graphic as a mark-bearing surface, and the elementary graphic sign as the smallest meaningful unit combining a visual variable with a spatial position; the figure-ground and visual-layer concepts where complex graphics decompose into readable layers; the construction rules where elementary signs combine under systematic principles rather than arbitrary arrangement) as the framework base; the retinal variables — the visual variable system: the complete variable inventory (position on the plane as the primary variable with unmatched precision and universality; size as the quantitative magnitude variable; value or lightness as the ordered intensity variable carrying the natural zero-to-maximum reading; color hue as the categorical discrimination variable with no inherent order; orientation as the directional variable; shape as the categorical form variable with limited capacity; and texture or grain as the density-pattern variable, each variable analyzed for its properties, capacity, and appropriate data types); the variable property analysis (the associative-dissociative-selective dimensions determining whether variables group, separate, or isolate information for different reading tasks; the quantitative-ordered-qualitative suitability of each variable for measurement, order, and category data respectively; the variable capacity limits, where hue distinguishes roughly seven categories while value supports fewer ordered steps, grounding the palette-capacity disciplines of the color traditions); the length-of-visual-variable concept (the range of discriminable steps within each variable, where position is effectively unlimited while shape is severely limited, driving encoding decisions for cardinality); the variable interaction effects (the combination and interference phenomena where simultaneous variables can reinforce, mask, or confuse each other; the construction-of-tables analysis where row-column arrangements of variables create systematic figure families; and the modern validation status where perception research has confirmed, refined, and occasionally corrected Bertin's original rankings, particularly the Cleveland-McGill elementary perceptual task hierarchy that reordered position, length, and angle precision) as the variable system; the levels of reading — the task hierarchy: the three reading levels (the elementary level reading individual sign values one at a time, the intermediate level comparing signs and identifying groups within a single layer, and the overall level perceiving global structure and relationships across the whole image; the design implication that graphics must support all three levels simultaneously, where a map readable point-by-point may fail at pattern level and vice versa); the reading-level-to-question mapping (the elementary level answering what-value questions about individual items, the intermediate answering comparison and grouping questions, and the overall answering structural and pattern questions; the question-first design method where the primary analytic question determines the required reading level and therefore the graphic construction); the layer and decomposition strategies (the multi-layer construction where different variables carry different information layers, each readable at its own level; the figure decomposition where complex questions split into series of simpler graphics each optimized for one reading level; and the small-multiples logic as systematic decomposition preserving comparison capability across panels); and the modern application (the reading levels in dashboard design where overview panels serve overall reading and detail panels serve elementary reading, connecting to information-architecture traditions; the level support in interactive systems where zoom and filter transitions move readers between levels deliberately) as the reading layer; the Bertin applications — maps, matrices, and diagrams: the cartographic applications (the thematic mapping system where retinal variables encode data on geographic position, the direct lineage to modern choropleth and proportional symbol maps; the map construction rules including the visual variable selection by data type and the legend as the decoding key, systems still governing map design; the surface and network cartography extensions from the semiology tradition); the matrix and table graphics (the table-network-diagram continuum where relational data finds graphic forms ordered by construction principle; the reorderable matrix concept where row-column sorting reveals block structure invisible in arbitrary order, the ancestor of modern clustered heatmaps and seriation methods; the graph and network layout as position-variable optimization problems); the flow and movement graphics (the stock-and-flow representations with their variable mappings; the movement-on-map conventions using arrows and trajectory lines with orientation and shape variables; the modern descendant systems from Sankey diagrams to animation-based flow visualization); and the legacy assessment (the influence traceable through Tufte's data-ink principles, the Cleveland perceptual experiments, the Mackinlay automated design theory of section 4, and the Munzner nested model's variable treatment; the known limitations including the static-print origin predating interaction and animation, the color science of its era superseded by modern appearance models, and the underdeveloped treatment of text and annotation as sign systems) as the applications layer; the operational Bertin method — the working discipline: the encoding decision procedure (the data-type-to-variable mapping as the first filter, where quantitative ordered data takes position, length, or value while categorical data takes hue or shape; the reading-level check asking whether the chosen encoding supports the primary question's required level; the capacity check verifying that data cardinality fits variable capacity limits; and the layer-conflict check ensuring simultaneous variables reinforce rather than mask the intended readings); the graphic construction audit (the systematic review of any visualization against variable properties, reading levels, and construction rules, extending the semiotic analysis method of section 1 with Bertin's rigor; the common failure detection including order carried by unordered variables, categories carried by ordered variables beyond discrimination capacity, and pattern questions answered with elementary-level-only encodings); the reexpression strategies (the transformation and reorganization options when initial constructions fail, from Bertin's systematic construction-of-tables exploration; the modern equivalents in view manipulation, aggregation, and faceting strategies from the information-architecture traditions); and the Bertin-plus-modern synthesis (the integration with perceptual science where Cleveland-McGill rankings refine variable precision ordering; the extension to interaction where reading levels map onto overview-detail navigation and variable capacity extends through dynamic queries; and the persistence of core principles where position primacy, capacity limits, and level support remain design law across seven decades of technological change) as the operational layer; and the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the order-by-hue error, encoding ordered data with unordered color hues so readers cannot rank values, remedied by the variable-property mapping discipline; the category-by-value confusion, distinguishing categories with lightness steps beyond discrimination capacity, remedied by the capacity-check procedure; the pattern-question-elementary-answer mismatch, asking readers to see global structure in encodings supporting only point reading, remedied by the reading-level-to-question mapping; the variable-masking overload, stacking simultaneous variables that interfere and destroy all intended readings, remedied by the layer-conflict audit; the legend-dependency design, building graphics requiring constant key consultation instead of direct or redundant encoding, remedied by the construction rules and labeling craft; the Bertin-fundamentalism, applying 1967 static-print rules dogmatically to interactive and animated contexts without extension, remedied by the modern-synthesis integration; and the aesthetic-override, letting style preferences defeat variable-selection logic, remedied by the decision-procedure discipline with detection methods as the diagnostic.

**Absorbed Skill:** Apply Bertin's graphical semiology through the framework (graphic information system thesis with monosemy goal, three-questions organization, elementary sign anatomy and layer decomposition), master the retinal variables (complete inventory of position, size, value, hue, orientation, shape, and texture with property analysis across associative-dissociative-selective and quantitative-ordered-qualitative dimensions, capacity limits and length-of-variable concepts, interaction effects and construction of tables, Cleveland-McGill modern validation), operate the reading levels (elementary-intermediate-overall hierarchy, question-to-level mapping driving construction, layer decomposition and small multiples strategies, dashboard and interaction applications), extend across applications (thematic mapping systems, reorderable matrices as clustered heatmap ancestors, flow and movement graphics, legacy influence and known limitations), run the operational method (encoding decision procedure with data-type mapping and capacity checks, construction audits detecting order-by-hue and level mismatches, reexpression strategies, Bertin-plus-modern synthesis), and avoid order-by-hue, category-by-value, level mismatch, variable masking, legend dependency, fundamentalism, and aesthetic override failures.

**Section 4 — Modern Encoding Theory: From Perceptual Hierarchies to Design Models**

**Summary:** Covers the scientific and computational descendants of Bertin that turn semiotic principles into validated, implementable encoding theory: the perceptual experiment tradition — measuring encoding effectiveness: the Cleveland-McGill hierarchy (the controlled-experiment program ranking elementary graphical perceptions by accuracy, establishing position along aligned scales first, then position along non-aligned scales, length, direction, angle and slope, area, volume and curvature, and shading and color saturation last; the experiment methodology where subjects make ratio judgments on encoded quantities, and the replication-and-refinement history including the Heer-Bostock crowdsourced reproductions that confirmed the broad ranking with nuances; the practical application where the hierarchy ranks encoding choices for quantitative accuracy tasks, with the important caveat that rankings are task-dependent and lower-ranked channels may serve other goals such as salience or aesthetics); the Stevens psychophysics foundation (the power-law relationships between physical magnitude and perceived intensity underlying channel scaling decisions, where brightness and saturation perception follow nonlinear laws requiring corrected color ramps; the perceptual uniformity concept driving modern colormaps where equal data steps produce equal perceived steps, the principle behind the viridis family from the color traditions); the channel capacity research (the discriminable-steps measurements quantifying how many levels each channel reliably carries, grounding Bertin's length-of-variable in experimental numbers; the categorical channel limits for hue, shape, and dash patterns with the seven-plus-or-minus-two working memory connections from the cognitive traditions; the interaction and interference findings where combined channels behave non-additively); and the modern extensions (the 3D and immersive encoding research where depth, motion, and spatial channels extend the hierarchy for VR and volumetric contexts at awareness; the animated and dynamic encoding studies where motion channels carry direction and change information with their own accuracy profiles; the crowd and field validation methods where large-scale online experiments test encodings in ecological conditions) as the experimental layer; the effectiveness-expressiveness framework — Mackinlay's APT: the formalization achievement (the Automatic Programming Tool theory where visualization design becomes a search problem over graphical languages, ranked by expressiveness and effectiveness criteria; the expressiveness criterion requiring that a graphic convey exactly the information in the data, no more and no less, formalizing the honesty requirement; the effectiveness criterion ranking graphics by how well their encodings match the perceptual hierarchy for the task at hand); the graphical language construction (the propositional representation of data relations and the graphical expression alternatives, where the same data relation can be expressed in multiple visual languages each with different effectiveness profiles; the composition operators combining graphical expressions into complete displays, the ancestor of modern compositional visualization grammars; the cost-model thinking where design decisions become computable rankings rather than taste judgments); the legacy and influence (the descendant systems from ShowMe and Tableau's automatic chart recommendation to Vega-Lite's smart defaults and recommendation engines, where APT principles operate inside commercial and open tools; the smart-default literature where automated systems encode data types to channels following the effectiveness hierarchy; the recommendation limitations awareness where automated tools optimize measured accuracy while missing rhetorical, aesthetic, and contextual factors that human designers manage); and the framework's semiotic reading (the expressiveness criterion as the denotation-fidelity requirement from the semiotics of section 1; the effectiveness criterion as the reader-competence optimization, matching encodings to human perceptual capabilities) as the APT layer; the Munzner nested model — the visualization design space: the four-level structure (the domain characterization level establishing what data and tasks the visualization serves; the data abstraction level defining data types and task taxonomies in formal terms; the encoding and interaction level where visual channels and manipulation techniques realize the abstractions; and the algorithm level where implementation choices execute the designs efficiently; the level-separation discipline where each level validates independently, preventing solution-first thinking that skips problem characterization); the task taxonomy (the why-what-how question framework where analysis targets discover why questions, search identifies what targets, and query answers how questions about data; the action-target pairs organizing exploration, browsing, and lookup behaviors with their object granularity from attributes to features to networks; the application to narrative visualization where scrollytelling guides readers through prescribed task sequences, connecting to the narrative course traditions); the channel taxonomy synthesis (the magritte channels dividing magnitude, identity, and spatial region information types with their subchannel rankings, unifying the Cleveland-McGill and Stevens traditions into one usable table; the accuracy-contrast-separation usage dimensions determining which channels suit which purposes, resolving the task-dependence caveat of raw rankings; the free-choice principles where non-accuracy goals legitimately claim lower-ranked channels); and the nested model as design reasoning tool (the top-down derivation discipline starting from domain and task before touching pixels, the antidote to the chart-first anti-pattern; the level-traceability audit where every encoding decision traces upward to a task justification, extending the semiotic analysis method of section 1) as the nested-model layer; the semiotics-science synthesis — the integrated theory: the mapping between traditions (the retinal variables and perceptual channels as the same entities described in semiotic and psychophysical vocabularies; the reading levels and task taxonomies as complementary frameworks for characterizing what graphics must support; the monosemy goal and expressiveness criterion as the shared honesty ideal in different formalizations); the complementary strengths (semiotics contributing the meaning-context-culture dimensions that experiments control away, where connotation and convention effects resist laboratory isolation; perceptual science contributing validated measurements that semiotic analysis can only hypothesize; the design-model synthesis where APT and nested models operationalize both into procedures); the unresolved frontiers (the aesthetic-quantification problem where beauty and engagement resist effectiveness formalization, previewing section 13's aesthetics debates; the cultural-variable integration where perceptual rankings assume Western-trained observers, previewing section 10's cross-cultural analysis; the narrative-and-rhetoric gap where persuasive and storytelling goals exceed accuracy-optimization frameworks, previewing section 11's rhetorical traditions); and the practitioner integration protocol (the four-step method of characterizing domain and tasks, abstracting data and questions, selecting channels by nested-model reasoning with semiotic connotation checks, and validating through both perceptual expectations and reader testing; the theory-triangulation habit where encoding decisions consult Bertin's properties, the perceptual hierarchies, and the design models together, with disagreement resolved by testing) as the synthesis layer; and the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the hierarchy dogmatism, mechanically applying channel rankings while ignoring task, salience, and aesthetic requirements, remedied by the free-choice principles and usage-dimension reasoning; the experiment overreach, generalizing laboratory rankings to contexts their methods never tested, remedied by the ecological-validation awareness; the expressiveness violation, shipping graphics asserting information the data does not contain or omitting what it does, remedied by the expressiveness-criterion audit; the solution-first design, choosing attractive chart types before characterizing domain and tasks, remedied by the nested-model top-down discipline; the automation abdication, accepting smart defaults without examining their effectiveness assumptions against actual tasks, remedied by the recommendation-limits literacy; the science-only blindness, optimizing measured accuracy while connotation, culture, and rhetoric silently distort meaning, remedied by the semiotics-science synthesis protocol; and the framework-fanaticism, treating any single model as complete design theory, remedied by the theory-triangulation habit with detection methods as the diagnostic.

**Absorbed Skill:** Command modern encoding theory through the perceptual experiment tradition (Cleveland-McGill hierarchy with replication history and task-dependence caveats, Stevens psychophysics driving perceptual uniformity, channel capacity measurements with cognitive connections, modern 3D-animation-crowd extensions), apply the APT framework (expressiveness and effectiveness criteria formalizing honesty and reader match, graphical language construction with composition operators, smart-default descendants and recommendation limits, semiotic reading of the criteria), wield the Munzner nested model (four-level structure with separation discipline, why-what-how task taxonomy with action-target pairs, magritte channel taxonomy with accuracy-contrast-separation dimensions, top-down derivation and traceability audits), synthesize semiotics with science (tradition mappings across variables-channels and reading-levels-tasks, complementary strengths with honest frontier acknowledgment, four-step practitioner integration protocol with theory triangulation), and avoid hierarchy dogmatism, experiment overreach, expressiveness violations, solution-first design, automation abdication, science-only blindness, and framework fanaticism failures.

**Section 5 — Metaphor Theory and Cognition: How Metaphor Structures Thought and Graphics**

**Summary:** Covers the cognitive science of metaphor that grounds visual metaphor design in how human understanding actually works: the conceptual metaphor revolution — Lakoff and Johnson: the metaphor-we-live-by thesis (the 1980 finding that metaphor pervades ordinary thought and language rather than decorating poetry, where everyday reasoning runs on structures like argument-is-war, time-is-money, and ideas-are-food; the conceptual mapping definition where understanding one domain, the target, in terms of another, the source, with systematic correspondences between their elements; the entailment principle where each metaphor licenses inferences that shape what we notice and ignore about the target); the primary metaphor foundations (the embodied origins of basic mappings from sensorimotor experience, where more-is-up arises from observing piles grow upward, affection-is-warmth from being held, and important-is-big from interacting with larger things, from the Grady primary-metaphor analysis; the cross-cultural partial-universality of primary metaphors grounded in shared embodiment versus cultural variation in complex metaphors built from them, previewing section 10; the natural-mapping advantage in interface design where primary-metaphor-aligned controls require less learning, connecting to section 1's motivated-sign concept); the orientational and ontological categories (the orientational metaphors organizing concepts spatially, where up-down, in-out, and front-back structures carry value judgments such as happy-is-up and control-is-up;
